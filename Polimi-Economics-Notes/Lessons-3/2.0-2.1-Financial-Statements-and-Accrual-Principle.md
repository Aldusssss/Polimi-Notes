# Lessons 2.0-2.1: Financial Statements and the Accrual Principle

## Topic Overview

Annual financial statements and the accrual principle (IFRS/OIC). The lessons introduce the annual financial statements as a tool of external accountability, describing the IFRS documents, annual and interim reporting, consolidated statements, and the differences between the economic perspective (profit) and the financial perspective (cash flow). They explain the stakeholders, the preparation/approval process, the OIC and IAS/IFRS accounting standards, and the accrual principle with practical examples of invoicing, collections and depreciation.

---

## Key Points Summary

1. Annual financial statements as the company's main tool of external accountability
2. Focus of the financial statements on economic, financial and equity data
3. Financial statements built according to precise rules set by laws and regulations
4. Readers of the financial statements: shareholders, public administration, banks and lenders, staff, customers
5. Approval of the financial statements: prepared by the board of directors, approved by the General Meeting of shareholders
6. Annual financial statements, with possible interim publications: half-yearly and quarterly for large and listed companies
7. Importance of the financial statements in representing the economic-financial and equity performance to different stakeholders
8. Ability to remunerate the invested capital and distribute dividends
9. Analysis by banks and lenders of the ability to repay loans
10. Role of the public administration: taxes and contribution to local economic development

**Key Quote:**

## Financial Statements and External Accountability

The annual financial statements are the formal tool through which the company represents its economic, financial and equity situation to the outside. They are the main tool of external accountability, that is, the way in which the company accounts for its activity and results to external parties. They focus on economic-financial and equity data, excluding non-accounting information (e.g. size characteristics or sector), and follow rules defined by laws and regulations.

**Key Points:**
- **Function:** account to external parties for the economic-financial and equity performance
- **Focus:** economic, financial and equity data, not other qualitative non-accounting data
- **Rules:** structure and preparation defined by legal provisions and regulations
- **Universal readability:** the financial statements of any company are comparable because they follow common rules

**Explanation:**
The lesson introduces the annual financial statements as a standardized language for the company's external communication. It clarifies that their purpose is to provide a synthetic but rigorous picture of performance and equity position, built on accounting principles and rules that ensure comparability and legal compliance.

**Considerations:**
- Ensure compliance with the rules to guarantee comparability and external validity
- Avoid mixing non-accounting qualitative data into the formal structure of the financial statements
- Maintain consistency of representation between financial years to allow trend analysis

---

## Stakeholders of the Financial Statements and Their Information Needs

Different classes of stakeholders read the financial statements with specific information needs: shareholders, public administration, banks and lenders, staff, customers. Shareholders approve the financial statements at the General Meeting; the board of directors prepares them. Banks and lenders assess solvency; the public administration assesses the ability to pay taxes and the contribution to development; customers and employees assess reliability and stability.

**Key Points:**
- **Shareholders:** approve the financial statements at the General Meeting; assess dividends and remuneration of capital
- **Board of directors:** technically prepares the financial statements
- **Public administration:** checks the ability to pay taxes and the contribution to local development
- **Banks/lenders:** analyze the ability to repay loans based on performance
- **Customers:** observe the ability to produce goods/services that satisfy needs
- **Employees:** assess economic-financial stability and the ability to pay wages and salaries

**Explanation:**
The lesson details that the financial statements are a multi-stakeholder information tool, each stakeholder with a different focus (remuneration, solvency, taxation, operating reliability). The formal process provides for preparation by the board of directors and approval by the shareholders.

**Considerations:**
- Clarify the preparation and approval process for governance transparency
- Present information that responds to different needs without confusing the economic and financial levels

## Annual and Interim Financial Statements

The annual financial statements are typically prepared/approved once a year, conventionally at the end of the financial year. In most companies the financial year coincides with the calendar year, but not necessarily. Large and listed companies must also publish financial statements on a half-yearly and quarterly basis.

**Key Points:**
- **Annual financial statements:** prepared and approved once a year
- **Financial year:** often coincides with the calendar year, but this is not mandatory
- **Listed and large companies:** half-yearly and quarterly publications during the year

**Explanation:**
The standard annual frequency is distinguished from the interim reporting required for transparency in the financial markets.

**Considerations:**
- Clearly specify the frequency and the time span covered by the documents
- For listed companies, ensure the timeliness of the interim publications

## Groups of Companies and Consolidated Financial Statements

When companies are organized in groups, with a parent company holding controlling stakes in subsidiaries, the correct perimeter of analysis is the group as a whole. The consolidated financial statements aggregate the economic, financial and equity results of all the companies of the group as a single entity.

**Key Points:**
- **Unit of analysis:** the company as a whole; for groups, the unit is the group
- **Parent company:** the main company that holds controlling stakes
- **Consolidated financial statements:** combine the financial statements of the parent company with those of all the subsidiaries
- Avoids the separate analysis of the individual financial statements when evaluating the group

**Explanation:**
The lesson clarifies the difference between separate and consolidated financial statements and the need for a single perimeter to faithfully represent the economic reality of the group.

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the consolidation perimeter and the equity interests
- Apply consolidation methods that comply with the accounting standards

## National and International Accounting Standards (OIC, IAS, IFRS)

In Italy the rules for preparing the financial statements are set by the Italian Accounting Body (OIC), made up of accountants, scholars and experts, which issues the national accounting standards. At the international level the IAS (International Accounting Standards) and IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) are adopted, in a process of harmonization to allow comparability between countries.

**Key Points:**
- **OIC:** technical body that issues the national accounting standards
- **National standards:** compliance with the Civil Code and legislation
- **IAS:** international accounting standards
- **IFRS:** way of preparing and presenting the financial statements at the international level
- **IAS/IFRS obligation:** listed companies; good practice for large companies even if not required
- **Harmonization:** makes it easier to read the financial statements of companies in different countries

**Explanation:**
The regulatory framework is outlined: national (OIC, Civil Code) and international (IAS/IFRS), highlighting who must adopt what and why standardization is crucial.

**Considerations:**
- Verify the obligations to adopt IAS/IFRS for listed companies at the European and international level
- For companies not required to, assess voluntary adoption for alignment and transparency

## Input-Output Model and Definition of the Economic Result

The company uses inputs (human, technological, financial resources) to transform them into outputs (products and services) that are sold. The economic result can be defined as the difference between the value of the output sold and the value of the resources used to produce it.

**Key Points:**
- **Input:** human, technological, financial resources
- **Output:** products and services sold
- **Economic result:** value of output – value of resources used

**Explanation:**
A simplified model is introduced to understand the logic of the income statement and the measurement of results.

**Considerations:**
- Clearly distinguish between economic value and cash flows when evaluating the result

---

## Economic Perspective: Profit for the Year (Revenues – Costs)

The economic perspective measures the result as the difference between revenues and costs. Revenues: the value of sales of goods and services in the period, not necessarily what was collected. Costs: the value of the resources used, not necessarily cash outflows. Profit for the year is revenues – costs.

**Key Points:**
- **Revenues:** sale value of the goods/services of the period, independent of the related collections
- **Costs:** value of the resources used, independent of the related cash outflows
- **Profit for the year:** revenues – costs
- **Focus:** economic accrual, not cash

**Explanation:**
The accrual principle (economic) is distinguished from the cash principle, showing how profit reflects economic performance net of the timing of monetary flows.

**Considerations:**
- Correctly apply the accrual principle to revenues and costs

---

## Financial Perspective: Net Cash Flow

The financial perspective measures the difference between cash inflows and cash outflows of the year. The net cash flow represents the financial result of the period and focuses on the liquidity generated/absorbed.

**Key Points:**
- Cash inflows from sales: do not necessarily coincide with revenues
- Cash outflows for resources: do not necessarily coincide with costs
- **Net cash flow:** cash inflows – cash outflows
- **Focus:** liquidity dynamics during the year

**Explanation:**
The lesson contrasts the financial measure with the economic one to highlight the importance of liquidity in assessing operating sustainability and the ability to self-finance.

**Considerations:**
- Separate the analysis of profitability (profit) from the analysis of liquidity (cash flow) for financial decisions

## Structure of the IFRS Financial Statements: 5 Main Documents

The IFRS financial statements consist of a plurality of combined documents: 1) Balance sheet (equity position); 2) Income statement (economic result of the year); 3) Statement of changes in equity; 4) Cash flow statement (cash inflows and outflows of the year); 5) Notes to the financial statements (technical information on the items).

**Key Points:**
- **Balance sheet:** equity position at the financial statements date
- **Income statement:** economic performance of the year
- **Statement of changes in equity:** movements of own capital
- **Cash flow statement:** cash flows by period
- **Notes to the financial statements:** technical details and criteria on the items
- **Management report:** qualitative and quantitative introduction to the context, highlights and strategic choices

**Explanation:**
The function of each document is listed and qualified, clarifying the integration between the economic, equity, financial and qualitative information dimensions.

**Considerations:**
- Ensure consistency between the accounting documents and the explanatory notes
- The management report must help interpret the accounting data

## Obligations to Adopt IAS/IFRS and OIC Standards

The International Accounting Standards do not have to be adopted by all companies: the obligation concerns listed companies. It is good practice for the larger companies to adopt them even if not required. The other companies follow the specific OIC accounting standards and the rules of the Civil Code.

**Key Points:**
- **Listed companies:** IAS/IFRS obligation
- **Large unlisted companies:** adoption recommended (good practice)
- **Other companies:** follow the OIC and the Civil Code

**Explanation:**
The subjective scope of application of the international standards compared with the national principles is clarified, in order to frame the accounting choices correctly.

**Considerations:**
- Verify listing status and size to determine the set of applicable principles

## Economic Accrual Principle

Accounting rule according to which the effects of transactions and operations of a management nature are recognized in the financial statements of the year in which the economic event occurs, regardless of the related cash flows. It underlies the preparation of the Balance Sheet and Income Statement and explains the non-coincidence between revenues/cash inflows and costs/cash outflows.

**Key Points:**
- Effects are recognized on the date of economic accrual, not at the time of collection/payment
- Revenues are recorded in the year in which the economic event occurs (e.g. issue of the invoice), even if the collection happens later
- Costs are recognized when they are reflected in a revenue (cause-effect relationship)
- Multi-year costs are spread over several years through depreciation
- Transactions affect the Balance Sheet and the Income Statement simultaneously through double-entry bookkeeping

**Explanation:**
The lesson clarifies that every management event generates an economic event (the moment in which the transaction produces legal-economic effects) and a financial event (the moment in which the monetary exchange takes place). Since these events often do not coincide in time, economic accrual requires revenues and costs to be recognized in the period in which they mature economically. For example, a sale with an invoice issued in December of year T generates the revenue in T, while the collection in T+1 concerns the cash flow statement. Costs are recorded when they can be linked to revenues, guaranteeing the matching principle: costs that contribute to the production of the goods/services sold are charged to the year of the revenue. For multi-year assets (warehouses, machinery, vehicles), their value is allocated over several years according to economic usefulness (depreciation). Support costs not directly linked to specific revenues (e.g. administrative salaries) are recorded in the year in which they are incurred. Recording takes place simultaneously on the Balance Sheet (changes in assets, liabilities, equity) and on the Income Statement (revenues and costs), using the double-entry technique.

**Example 1:**
A company sells products in December of year T and issues the invoice in December (economic event). The customer pays in January of year T+1 (financial event). The revenue is recorded in the Income Statement of year T; the cash inflow is recorded in the Cash Flow Statement of year T+1.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identification of the economic event: invoice issued in December of year T
- Recognition of the revenue: year T in the Income Statement
- Identification of the financial event: collection in January of year T+1
- Recognition of the cash flow: year T+1 in the Cash Flow Statement
- Equity effects: trade receivables at the end of year T, reduction of the receivable and increase in cash in T+1

**Example 2:**
The company acquires a machine worth 10 and uses it for 5 years. The value of 10 is spread over the 5 years of use, charging an annual cost of 2 (10 divided by 5) to the Income Statement, with a parallel reduction in the value of the asset in the Balance Sheet.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Determination of the useful life: 5 years
- Calculation of the annual amount: 10 divided by 5 = 2
- Annual recognition: depreciation cost of 2 in the Income Statement
- Equity recognition: decrease in the carrying value of the machine by 2 per year
- Consistency with economic accrual: allocation of the cost according to the multi-year usefulness

**Considerations:**
- Ensure the correct identification of the economic event for each transaction
- Ensure the cause-effect correlation between costs and revenues
- Spread the costs of multi-year assets through depreciation according to economic usefulness
- Separate economic events from financial events in the accounts when they do not coincide
- Apply double-entry bookkeeping for simultaneous recognition on the Balance Sheet and Income Statement
- Record cash flows systematically and in parallel for the preparation of the Cash Flow Statement
- Record support costs in the year in which they are incurred if they cannot be directly linked to revenues
- Verify that the accounting year covers 12 months and define the dates correctly (1 January – 31 December, if it coincides with the calendar year)

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the invoice is issued at the end of the year and the collection in the new year, how is it recorded? The revenue is recognized in the year of invoicing (T) and the cash flow in the following year (T+1), with a receivable from customers at the end of T
- If a cost cannot be attributed to a specific revenue, how is it treated? It is recorded in full in the year in which it is incurred, without deferral
- If an asset has multi-year usefulness, how is the cost charged? The asset is capitalized and depreciated over several years, allocating annual amounts consistent with the useful life
- If the financial year does not coincide with the calendar year, how is it managed? The 12-month duration is maintained and the accrual principles are applied to the specific dates of the financial year

## Financial Cash Principle (Cash Perspective)

Accounting perspective focused on recording cash flows (inflows and outflows) when they actually occur. It underlies the Cash Flow Statement.

**Key Points:**
- Records collections and payments at the moment they occur
- Does not determine revenues and costs, but operating, investing and financing flows
- Can diverge in time from economic accrual
- Aggregation of the flows at year-end in the Cash Flow Statement

**Explanation:**
The cash perspective records positive (inflows) and negative (outflows) financial flows as they occur over the 12 months of the financial year. These flows include collections from sales, payments for purchases of materials, collections of receivables and payments of debts, as well as purchases and disposals of assets. At the end of the year, these movements are aggregated in the Cash Flow Statement, which gives a view of liquidity and of the ability to generate cash.

**Example:**
During the year, cash inflows are recorded for collections of receivables and cash outflows for payments of debts. These movements do not directly change the recognition of revenues/costs in the Income Statement if they have already been recorded on an accrual basis, but they change the cash and receivable/payable items.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Collection of receivables: increase in cash, decrease in receivables
- Payment of debts: decrease in cash, decrease in payables
- Cash Flow Statement: classification of the flows into the respective segments (operating/financing/investing)

**Considerations:**
- Promptly record every inflow/outflow for the accuracy of the Cash Flow Statement
- Align the cash flows with the corresponding equity items (receivables, payables, fixed assets)
- Distinguish operating, investing and financing flows

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a cash flow is linked to a transaction already recognized economically in a previous year, how is it treated? It is recorded in the Cash Flow Statement of the current period, updating cash and the related equity item (e.g. receivables/payables)

## Structure of the Financial Statements and Time Horizon

The annual financial statements cover a period of 12 months called the accounting year. In most cases it coincides with the calendar year (from 1 January to 31 December), but it may not, while still lasting 12 months. It includes the Balance Sheet, the Income Statement and the Cash Flow Statement.

**Key Points:**
- **Duration of the financial year:** 12 months
- Typical alignment with the calendar year: 1 January – 31 December
- **Documents:** Balance Sheet, Income Statement (economic accrual), Cash Flow Statement (cash)
- **Relationships between documents:** production and sales link the economic side and the equity side; cash records the flows

**Explanation:**
The definition of the financial year is fundamental for establishing the timing of the operations. Most companies adopt the calendar year, but some define a different financial year, always 12 months long. The Balance Sheet represents resources and their uses; the Income Statement represents the result of production and sales activity; the Cash Flow Statement summarizes cash inflows and outflows. The lesson stresses that these perspectives are integrated but distinct in their recognition rules.

**Example:**
If a company adopts the calendar year, the 2025 financial year starts on 1 January 2025 and ends on 31 December 2025. Transactions occurring between these dates are allocated according to the economic accrual principle for the Balance Sheet and Income Statement and according to the cash perspective for the Cash Flow Statement.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Definition of the dates: 1 January 2025 – 31 December 2025
- Classification of the transactions: economic event vs. financial event
- Alignment of the documents at the end of the year for the preparation of the financial statements

**Considerations:**
- Establish exactly the start and end of the financial year for correct allocation
- Coordinate the administrative teams for closings and reconciliations between documents
- Provide for transactions straddling two financial years and treat them correctly
