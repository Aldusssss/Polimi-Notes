# Lessons 2.2-2.3: Balance Sheet, Fair Value and Depreciation

## Topic Overview

Balance sheet, fair value, depreciation. Summary of the lessons on the definition and structure of the Balance Sheet, the classification of assets and liabilities, equity, provisions and severance pay (TFR). It covers valuation at cost and at fair value, impairment, inventories, tangible and intangible fixed assets, goodwill in M&A, and the distinction between current and non-current liabilities, with the differences between Italian standards and IFRS.

1. Definition and structure of the Balance Sheet (two sections: Assets on the left, Liabilities and Equity on the right)
2. Conventional reference date: 31 December of year T (last day of the financial year)
3. Fundamental relationship: Total Assets = Total Liabilities and Equity
4. Liabilities and Equity side: claims on the resources (own funds and third-party funds)
5. Components of the Liabilities and Equity side: Equity, Non-current liabilities, Current liabilities

7. Non-current liabilities: bonds outstanding, bank debt, other financial liabilities, TFR, provisions for risks and charges
8. Current liabilities: current financial liabilities, trade payables, tax payables

## Balance Sheet: Definition and Structure

Document of the financial statements that photographs the company's equity position at a given moment (conventionally 31 December of the reference year). It is made up of two opposing sections: Assets (left) and Liabilities and Equity (right). Total Assets equals Total Liabilities and Equity.

**Key Points:**
- Conventional reference moment: 31 December of year T of the financial year
- Two sections: Liabilities and Equity (claims on the resources) and Assets (uses of the resources)
- Identity relationship: Total Assets = Total Liabilities and Equity
- **Liabilities and Equity side:** own capital (shareholders) and debt capital (banks, lenders)
- **Assets side:** resources used in the business (machinery, cash, inventories, etc.)

**Explanation:**
The Balance Sheet represents, at the conventional date of 31 December of the financial year, the set of sources (Liabilities and Equity) and uses (Assets). The Liabilities and Equity side describes who has claims on the resources (shareholders for equity, lenders for liabilities). The Assets side describes how those resources are used in the assets useful for production and management. The Assets = Liabilities and Equity parity derives from the fact that every resource has a party holding the claim on it.

**Example:**
Analyzing the Balance Sheet of a company for year T, the reference date is 31 December of year T. On the right (Liabilities and Equity) there are equity and liabilities; on the left (Assets) the uses. The sum of the Assets and of the Liabilities and Equity coincides.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the reference date: 31 December of year T
- Recognize the opposing sections: Assets and Liabilities and Equity
- Verify the Assets = Liabilities and Equity parity as a balancing check

## Liabilities and Equity: Equity, Non-Current Liabilities, Current Liabilities

The Liabilities and Equity side includes equity (share capital, reserves, profit for the year) and liabilities, divided into non-current (due beyond the next financial year) and current (due within the next financial year).

**Key Points:**
- **Equity (E):** initial capital and increases, reserves, profit for the year
- **Non-current liabilities:** bonds outstanding, bank debt, other financial liabilities, TFR, provisions for risks and charges
- **Current liabilities:** current financial liabilities, trade payables, tax payables
- The current/non-current distinction is based on the maturity relative to the next financial year

**Explanation:**
The classification of the Liabilities and Equity side makes it possible to understand the sources of financing and their timing of repayment. Equity indicates the contribution of the owners and the accumulation of retained results. The liabilities show the commitments to third parties, separating short-term from long-term ones and including elements typical of the Italian context such as TFR and provisions.

**Example:**
In equity: share capital (foundation and increases), reserves (undistributed profits), profit for the year. In non-current liabilities: bonds outstanding, bank debt, other financial liabilities, TFR, provisions for risks and charges. In current liabilities: current financial liabilities, trade payables, tax payables.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the equity items
- Separate the liabilities by maturity
- Recognize the specific features: TFR and provisions for risks/charges

**Considerations:**
- Always clarify the maturity of the liabilities for correct classification
- Highlight TFR as a non-current liability typical of the Italian context
- Separate financial and trade payables using time criteria

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a debt has components with different maturities, how should it be classified? Separate the portion due within the next financial year among the current liabilities and the remainder among the non-current ones

## Assets: Non-Current and Current

Assets represent the uses of the resources. They are divided into non-current assets (converted into cash beyond 1 year) and current assets (cash or converted within the next financial year).

**Key Points:**
- **Non-current assets:** plants, vehicles, patents, equity investments, other financial assets
- **Current assets:** cash, trade receivables, inventories, current financial assets
- **Civil Code scheme:** tangible, intangible and financial fixed assets; current assets
- Current financial assets held for sale within the next financial year (not for investment purposes)

**Explanation:**
The distinction between current and non-current assets reflects the time horizon of liquidation of the assets. Fixed assets have a multi-year useful life and are presented as tangible, intangible or financial. Current assets include cash, receivables from customers, stock and financial instruments held for short-term disposal.

**Example:**
Non-current assets: property, plant and equipment; investment property; intangible assets with an indefinite life; other intangible assets; equity investments; other financial assets. Current assets: trade receivables, inventories, current financial assets, cash.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map the international items to the Civil Code macro-items: tangible/intangible/financial fixed assets
- Verify the purpose and time horizon to classify the item as current or non-current

**Considerations:**
- Verify the purpose of holding the financial assets (investment vs. sale)
- Ascertain convertibility into cash within the next financial year to classify as current

**Special Circumstances:**
- If an intangible asset has an indefinite life, how should it be treated? Classify it among the non-current assets and assess depreciation/write-down according to the applicable standards (no depreciation if the life is indefinite, impairment test)

## Cash, Trade Receivables, Inventories, Current Financial Assets

Main components of current assets with operational definitions: cash in the bank, receivables from sales, stocks of materials and products, financial instruments intended for short-term sale.

**Key Points:**
- **Cash:** money held in the bank and at credit institutions
- **Trade receivables:** expected collections from customers within the next financial year
- **Inventories:** raw materials, semi-finished and finished goods not used or not sold in the year
- **Current financial assets:** equity investments or debt securities held for sale within the next financial year

**Explanation:**
These items are essential for the management of working capital. Correct measurement and time classification affect short-term liquidity and solvency.

## Valuation in the Financial Statements: Cost vs. Fair Value

When a management event is recorded (acquisition of a resource), the value in the financial statements can be recorded at purchase cost or at fair value (the fair market value) according to international accounting standards.

**Key Points:**
- **Cost model:** recording at the acquisition value (the traditional guiding principle in Italy)
- **Fair value:** exchange value between independent, knowledgeable and willing parties
- Need to clarify the criteria for determining fair value in the financial statements
- Fair value also applicable to liabilities (e.g. the value of debts)
- Fair value requires estimates and appraisals based on objective external values

**Explanation:**
The choice between cost and fair value depends on the representation that is closest to economic reality. When the book value based on cost does not adequately reflect the value of the asset, fair value supported by appraisals can be used. The financial statements must make the estimation criteria adopted transparent.

**Example:**
If a machine is bought at a price below market value (discount), the purchase cost differs from the fair value. The international standards allow valuation at fair value, clarifying in the financial statements the criteria and the appraisals used.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Comparison between cost and fair value
- Documentation of the estimation criteria and sources (appraisals)
- Effects on the representation of the value of the asset

**Considerations:**
- Explicitly state in the financial statements the fair value criteria used
- Ensure objectivity through appraisals and market data

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the market is illiquid and observable prices are missing, how should fair value be determined? Use valuation techniques based on observable inputs where possible and on accepted models, documenting the assumptions

## Determining Fair Value and Appraisals

The determination of fair value refers to the general market value and requires specific criteria and appraisals for different types of assets (land, buildings, plants, machinery).

**Key Points:**
- For land and buildings: specific market parameters and appraisals
- For plants and machinery: appraisals to determine the real market value
- Adoption of fair value when the book value at cost does not adequately represent the value of the asset

**Explanation:**
Companies revalue at fair value to improve the representation of their assets when historical cost loses significance. This implies recourse to experts and estimation methodologies suited to the asset.

## Tangible Fixed Assets: Recognition, Depreciation and Write-Downs

Assets for durable use, instrumental to production or administration. Recorded at initial cost, then presented net of accumulated depreciation and write-downs (fair value where applicable). Land is not depreciated.

**Key Points:**
- Examples: land, buildings, machinery, property, equipment, office machines
- Initial recording at the acquisition value
- Subsequent representation net of accumulated depreciation and any write-downs
- **Land:** not depreciated

**Explanation:**
Tangible fixed assets reflect the multi-year use of the assets. The reduction of the value in the financial statements year by year occurs through depreciation, while extraordinary changes can be reflected through write-downs or revaluations.

## Depreciation: Concept, Calculation and Duration

Depreciation is the portion of the value of a multi-year asset allocated to each year according to its economic useful life. The duration starts when the asset is available for use and ends when it no longer has a useful life.

**Key Points:**
- Allocation of the cost over the useful life
- Annual reduction of the book value to reflect economic use
- **Duration:** from the start of availability for use until the end of the useful life

**Explanation:**
Depreciation links the economic consumption of the asset to the periods that benefit from it, respecting the accrual principle. Its determination requires defining the useful life and the method of allocation (amount).

**Example:**
Machine with a value of 10 and a useful life of 5 years. Annual amount 2. Book values: after 1 year 8 (10-2), after 2 years 6 (10-2-2), and at the end of the 5 years 0.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Calculation of the annual amount: 10/5 = 2
- Updating the net book value year by year
- Reduction of the value to zero at the end of the useful life

**Considerations:**
- Correctly define the useful life and the depreciation method
- Exclude land from depreciation

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the useful life changes due to obsolescence or use, how should the company adjust? Review the estimates and update the depreciation plan prospectively

## Depreciation of Durable Assets

Depreciation is the accounting process of allocating the cost of a durable asset over its useful life, determined according to accounting standards that set rates for categories of fixed assets. It serves to correctly represent the residual value in the assets and to charge an annual cost to the income statement, affecting the result for the year.

**Key Points:**
- Not discretionary: determined by accounting standards and rates for types of assets
- **Depreciation methods:** constant amounts (prevalent for simplicity), amounts proportional to the residual value (decreasing), per unit of product (proportional to use)
- Correct determination is needed for the balance sheet (residual value net of depreciation) and for the income statement (cost for the year)
- It represents the use of the resource in a specific financial year

**Explanation:**
The instructor stresses that the duration and the rate of depreciation are defined by the accounting standards and not by the company's free choice. The constant-amount method spreads the cost in equal parts over the useful life; the method proportional to the residual value assigns decreasing amounts (assuming more intense use at the beginning); the per-unit-of-product method links the amount to the actual use. The annual amount reduces the book value of the asset in the assets and is a cost in the income statement of the year, contributing to the formation of the result.

**Example:**
A machine with a recorded total cost and a useful life defined by the standards is depreciated each year with the same amount, simplifying the accounting and ensuring a stable representation of the cost of use in the income statement.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the historical cost and the useful life according to the standards
- Calculate the annual amount = cost / years
- Record the amount as a cost and reduce the net value of the asset

**Considerations:**
- Choose the depreciation method most consistent with the usage profile of the asset and with accounting simplicity
- Periodically verify the useful life in light of the accounting standards and operating conditions

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the use of the asset varies significantly between years, how should it be treated? Consider the per-unit-of-product method to align the cost of use with the actual employment

## Intangible Fixed Assets and Differences Between Italian and International Standards

Intangible fixed assets are identifiable non-monetary assets without physical substance, controlled by the company and associated with future economic benefits (trademarks, patents, licenses, goodwill). The Italian standards allow the capitalization of some expenses (basic research, start-up, incorporation, training, advertising/marketing), which is prohibited by the international standards, which limit intangible assets to licenses, patents, trademarks and goodwill.

**Key Points:**
- **Definition:** identifiable intangible assets, controlled, with future benefits
- **Italian standards:** basic research, start-up costs, incorporation, staff training, advertising/marketing/promotion can be capitalized
- **International standards:** such costs cannot be capitalized; intangibles mainly licenses, patents, trademarks, goodwill

**Explanation:**
The instructor contrasts the Italian system with the international standards, underlining the different philosophy: in Italy some multi-year expenses can be capitalized (rationale: they create an asset of skills), whereas IFRS prohibit the capitalization of those types, out of prudence and verifiability of the future benefits.

**Example:**
In Italy, basic research costs can be capitalized and spread over several years. Under the international standards they must be expensed to the income statement in the year in which they are incurred.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the nature of the expense (basic research)
- Apply local rules or IFRS for the accounting treatment
- Document the rationale for capitalization (Italy) or expensing (IFRS)

**Considerations:**
- Document control and future benefits for the recognition of an intangible asset
- Adapt accounting policies to the reference framework (Italy vs. IFRS)

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a research project does not have a demonstrable probability of future benefits, how should it be treated? Expense the costs to the income statement according to the international standards

## Goodwill in Business Combinations

Goodwill arises in acquisitions or mergers as the difference between the acquisition cost and the acquirer's share of the current values of the assets, liabilities and contingent liabilities acquired at the transaction date. It reflects the growth potential and the expected economic value of the acquired company beyond the book values, and is recorded among the intangible assets.

**Key Points:**
- **Origin:** acquisition/merger
- **Calculation:** acquisition cost − share of the current values of the identifiable assets/liabilities/contingent liabilities
- **Rationale:** the price incorporates future potential, not only balance sheet values
- **Classification:** intangible asset recorded in the assets

**Explanation:**
The instructor explains that the balance sheet values reflect the company's past life, whereas the purchase price reflects growth expectations. The difference is recorded as goodwill, a durable intangible asset that represents expected future value and is recorded in the assets.

**Example:**
Company A acquires Company B at a price that exceeds the share of the current values of assets, liabilities and contingent liabilities. The difference is recorded as goodwill in A's assets.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Determine the fair value of B's assets/liabilities/contingent liabilities
- Calculate A's share of those values
- Calculate the difference with the acquisition cost
- Record the difference as goodwill

**Considerations:**
- Carry out accurate fair value valuations at the transaction date
- Document the contingent liabilities identified for the calculation of the difference

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the acquisition price is lower than the net fair value (badwill), how should it be treated? Recognize a gain from a bargain purchase according to the applicable standards

## Financial Assets: Categories and Impairment

Financial assets are classified into four categories: held for trading; investments held to maturity; trade and financial receivables; available for sale. All are subject to an impairment test to check the consistency between the book value and the market value at the date the balance sheet is prepared; if the book value is higher than the market value, the value is reduced and the loss is recognized as a cost for the year.

**Key Points:**
- **Categories:** trading; held to maturity; trade/financial receivables; available for sale
- **Impairment:** comparison of book value vs. market value at the preparation date
- If book value > market value: reduction and recognition of the loss as a cost

**Explanation:**
The instructor illustrates the need for the impairment test as an economic assessment: an example on an equity investment in Company B held by Company A. The value recorded in the financial statements is checked against the market value; any excess is reduced and the loss is recorded in the income statement.

**Example:**
Company A holds a share of Company B's capital. At the closing of the year, A compares the book value of the investment with the market value; if the book value is higher, A reduces the value and records the loss as a cost.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Determine the fair value/market value of the investment at the financial statements date
- Compare it with the book value
- Record the impairment if necessary and its impact on the income statement

**Considerations:**
- Establish reliable criteria for determining the market value at the financial statements date
- Apply consistent impairment policies across years

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the market is illiquid or not observable, how should it be treated? Use consistent valuation techniques (DCF, multiples) and document the assumptions

## Inventories: Definition, Recognition and Valuation

Inventories include goods held for sale in the ordinary course of business, semi-finished goods and unused raw materials. They are not recognized in the income statement until they generate revenue or are used in production. They are valued at the lower of cost (purchase or production) and net realizable value (estimated selling price for the next financial year).

**Key Points:**
- **Components:** unsold finished products, semi-finished goods, unused raw materials
- **Recognition:** in the assets of the balance sheet, not in the income statement until sale/use
- **Valuation:** lower of cost and net realizable value (estimated selling price in the next financial year)
- Need for an adequate internal accounting system to determine the values correctly

**Explanation:**
The instructor stresses that the lower of cost and net realizable value governs the recording of inventories. To estimate correctly, an internal accounting system is needed that tracks purchase/production costs and estimates of the selling price in the next financial year.

**Example:**
If a finished product has a production cost higher than the estimated selling price in the next financial year, the inventories are valued at the estimated price (net realizable value).

**Calculation Steps:**
- Determine the production cost
- Estimate the selling price in the next financial year
- Apply the lower of cost and net realizable value criterion

**Considerations:**
- Implement cost accounting to track production costs
- Periodically update the net realizable value estimates according to the market

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the inventories become obsolete, how should it be treated? Reduce the value to an updated net realizable value, possibly writing it off entirely if unsellable

## Assets Held for Disposal: Classification and Conditions

Assets held for disposal (part of the business to be sold in the next financial year) must be represented separately in the assets of the balance sheet if they are identifiable, measurable and separately valuable. Conditions: existence of a sale plan and a concrete program started with documentary evidence; promotion of the sale at a price that allows the exchange; sale likely within one year of the classification date (year T with sale within T+1).

**Key Points:**
- Separate representation in the assets for clarity and measurability
- **Conditions:** plan and program started; practicable price; sale within one year (T → T+1)
- Documentary evidence of the management's decision

**Explanation:**
The instructor describes the requirements for isolating and presenting assets held for disposal: the aim is to inform stakeholders about portions of the business that will be disposed of within defined times, with documentary support and realistic market conditions.

**Example:**
A company decides to sell a division. It draws up a sale plan, starts the program, promotes the sale at a market price, and expects to close within a year. In the financial statements of T, the division is presented separately in the assets as an asset held for disposal.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Formalize the plan and the documentation
- Verify the price and marketability
- Classify and present separately
- Monitor the timing (by T+1)

**Considerations:**
- Maintain documentary traceability of the decisions and of the actions taken for the sale
- Update the valuations in case of slippage in the timing

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the sale is no longer likely within one year, how should it be treated? Review the classification and reallocate the asset according to the ordinary criteria

## Structure of the Balance Sheet and Equity

The balance sheet consists of assets and liabilities and equity, prepared conventionally at 31 December of year T. Total assets coincide with total liabilities and equity. Assets are divided into current and non-current. The liabilities and equity side includes equity (share capital, reserves, retained earnings, profit for the year), current and non-current liabilities.

**Key Points:**
- **Reference date:** 31 December of year T
- Equivalence total assets = total liabilities and equity
- **Non-current assets:** tangible/intangible/financial fixed assets; **current assets:** inventories, cash, trade receivables
- Equity defined as residual (assets − liabilities), structured into share capital, reserves, retained earnings, profit for the year
- Need to integrate information in the notes to the financial statements and in the statement of changes in equity

**Explanation:**
The instructor goes through the formal structure, clarifying that the international standards leave local detail to the civil legislation. Equity is residual but includes structured items and requires additional disclosure.

**Example:**
A company prepares its financial statements under IFRS, detailing the reserves (legal, statutory, share premium, revaluation) on the basis of the Civil Code and the Italian standards, providing explanations in the notes and in the statement of changes.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the equity items
- Apply local rules for the detail
- Provide disclosure in the notes and in the statement of changes

**Considerations:**
- Align the equity items with local rules even within the international framework
- Ensure transparency with the notes and the statement of changes

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the company carries out a capital increase with a share premium, how should it be treated? Record the difference between the issue price and the nominal value in the share premium reserve

## Reserves and Their Purposes

Reserves represent resources intended for self-financing and the reinvestment of profits. Items provided for: share premium reserve (difference between the issue price and the nominal value), revaluation reserve (increases in the value of assets, often fixed assets, measured for example at fair value), legal reserve (a buffer required by law), statutory reserve (provided by the bylaws). Retained earnings are profits intended for reinvestment but not yet allocated.

**Key Points:**
- **Share premium:** difference between the market price in a capital increase and the nominal value
- **Revaluation:** accommodates increases in the value of fixed assets from valuation (e.g. fair value)
- **Legal reserve:** restricted by law
- **Statutory reserve:** restricted by the bylaws
- **Retained earnings:** awaiting specific allocation

**Explanation:**
The instructor illustrates the role of reserves as tools for strengthening the capital base and for reinvestment, highlighting the need for correct classification and disclosure.

**Example:**
Issue of new shares at a price above the nominal value: the difference is recorded in the share premium reserve.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Calculate the price − nominal value difference
- Record the difference in the reserve
- Update the statement of changes in equity

**Considerations:**
- Verify the legal and statutory restrictions on the use of the reserves
- Document the revaluations and the methodology (e.g. fair value)

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a revaluation is subsequently reduced, how should it be treated? Recognize the reduction in accordance with the standards, adjusting the revaluation reserve and the impacts on the income statement where provided

## Provisions (Provisions for Risks and Charges)

Under the international standards we speak of provisions: liabilities whose amount or timing is uncertain but whose necessity is determined. Conditions for recognition: 1) existence of a present obligation (legal or constructive) as a result of a past event; 2) need to use economic resources to settle it; 3) possibility of a reliable estimate of the amount required.

**Key Points:**
- **Terminology:** provisions (not "funds" in IFRS)
- Liabilities with uncertain amount/timing, certain necessity
- **Conditions:** present obligation; use of resources; reliable estimate
- Examples: legal disputes, certain future obligations with uncertainty over amount/timing

**Explanation:**
The instructor clarifies the prudential logic: liabilities are recognized when the company has a present obligation arising from past events and will likely have to incur outlays, and can estimate the amount. Provisions are reported in the liabilities (normally non-current if the maturity exceeds the next financial year).

**Example:**
The company has a dispute with a probable future outlay, but the amount and timing are not determined. An estimated provision is recognized in the financial statements of year T to meet the obligation that may arise in T+1.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Verify the existence of a present obligation (past event)
- Confirm the need for an outlay of resources
- Reliably estimate the amount
- Record the provision in the liabilities

**Considerations:**
- Gather documentary evidence and legal opinions for the estimate
- Update the provision estimates at every financial statements closing

**Special Circumstances:**
- If it is not possible to reliably estimate the amount, how should it be treated? Provide disclosure in the notes and do not recognize a provision until the estimate becomes reliable

## Funds and Provisions in the Liabilities of the Balance Sheet

Funds represent estimated liabilities for present obligations that will generate future economic outflows. They are recorded in the liabilities of the balance sheet and determined according to the best estimate required by the accounting standards, with details provided in the Notes.

**Key Points:**
- Provisions for termination indemnities for collaborations other than employment
- Provision for legal risks for regulatory or legal disputes
- Current tax provision for liabilities related to taxes and tax disputes
- Best estimate of the economic outflow as the valuation criterion under the accounting standards
- Obligation to disclose the estimation criteria in the Notes

**Explanation:**
The instructor explains that when the company foresees probable and estimable obligations, it must set aside resources in specific funds in the liabilities. For the termination indemnity of non-employee collaborations, an economic amount to be paid in the future is estimated and recorded as a liability. Similarly, for legal risks and current taxes, the accounting standards require the best estimate of the outflows needed. The methods and criteria adopted for the estimate must subsequently be set out in the Notes, ensuring transparency.

**Example:**
A company faces a regulatory dispute that could result in an outlay. Based on the legal opinion and the probability of loss, it estimates the potential charge and sets up a provision for legal risks, recording it in the liabilities and describing the estimation criteria in the Notes.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Assessment of the probability and of the estimated amount with legal advice
- Recording of the fund in the liabilities as a contingent liability
- Drafting of the disclosure in the Notes with the criteria and assumptions used

**Considerations:**
- Use the best possible estimate of the future economic outflow
- Support the estimates with documentary evidence and technical/legal opinions
- Ensure adequate disclosure in the Notes on the estimation criteria
- Periodically update the fund estimates based on new information

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the probability of an economic outflow cannot be sufficiently determined, how should it be handled? The fund is not recognized, but disclosure is provided in the Notes on the nature of the uncertainty and the possible implications

## Employee Benefit Funds and Severance Pay (T.F.R.)

Funds relating to personnel include employee benefits to be paid after the closing of the financial statements, such as bonuses and additional wages. T.F.R. is a post-employment benefit, with defined programs and benefits, which summarizes the amounts accrued and not yet paid, to be settled at the end of the employment relationship.

**Key Points:**
- **Post-closing benefits:** remuneration to be paid in the following year (bonuses, additional wages)
- Provisional recording as employee benefit funds
- T.F.R. as a post-employment benefit with predetermined amount and criteria
- Settlement of the T.F.R. at the end of the employment relationship, whether early or at term

**Explanation:**
Within the framework of the international accounting standards, employee benefits include all the amounts that will be paid after the closing date of the financial statements. Although the personnel cost is recorded in the income statement, future outflows such as bonuses and additional wages are set aside in the funds. T.F.R., specific to the Italian system, has the nature of defined benefits: the amount accrues over time according to predetermined criteria and is settled at the end of the employment relationship.

**Example:**
At the closing of the financial statements, the company expects to pay a bonus and accrued overtime in the following year. It sets aside the estimated amount in the employee benefit funds, separate from the personnel cost already recognized.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identification of the remuneration accrued but not paid
- Estimate of the amount to be paid in the following year
- Recording in the liabilities as an employee benefits fund

**Considerations:**
- Separate costs accruing to the year from future outflows to be set aside
- Apply predetermined criteria for the measurement of the T.F.R.
- Update the benefit estimates according to contracts and company policies

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the employment relationship ends prematurely, how should it be handled? Settle the T.F.R. accrued at the termination date according to the predetermined criteria and record any differences compared with the estimates

## Classification of Financial Liabilities: Current and Non-Current

Financial liabilities are divided into current (settled within the next financial year) and non-current (settled beyond the next financial year). The classification affects the structure of the liabilities and the assessment of liquidity and solvency.

**Key Points:**
- **Current liabilities:** maturity within the next financial year
- **Time reference:** if the balance sheet is at 31 December of year T, current debts are settled by 31 December of year T+1
- **Current categories:** bank debt, bonds maturing within the next financial year, trade payables, tax payables, payables to social security institutions, finance lease liabilities (short-term portion)
- **Non-current liabilities:** settlement beyond the next financial year
- **Analogous non-current categories:** bonds, bank debt, finance lease liabilities with maturity beyond the next financial year

**Explanation:**
The instructor clearly distinguishes the time dimension of financial liabilities. The current part includes all the obligations to be settled within the following financial year, including bank debt, bonds falling due, trade and tax payables, social security contributions and the short-term portion of finance leases. Non-current liabilities represent the same categories when the maturity is beyond the following financial year. The practical example of 31 December of year T clarifies the classification horizon: by 31 December of year T+1 for the current ones.

**Example:**
A mortgage with payments scheduled over the next 5 years: the portion falling due within the next financial year is classified as a current liability; the remaining portions as non-current liabilities.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Breakdown of the debt between current and non-current portions
- Verification of the contractual maturities against the financial statements date

## Structure of Liabilities and Equity: Equity (E) and Liabilities (Debts, D)

The liabilities and equity side of the balance sheet can be summarized in two blocks: equity or own capital (indicated as E) and liabilities to third parties (debts, indicated as D). The sum D plus E represents the total liabilities and equity of the balance sheet.

**Key Points:**
- Equity/own capital defined as equity, indicated as E
- Liabilities to third parties identified as D
- Total liabilities and equity equal to D plus E

**Explanation:**
The lesson proposes a useful summary: the liabilities and equity side is made up of the own capital contributed and generated by the company (equity, E) and the debt capital coming from third parties (D). The conceptual equality of total liabilities and equity to D+E helps in understanding the financial structure.

**Example:**
A company evaluates the structure of its liabilities and equity by considering the composition between D and E to determine leverage and the impacts on risk and return.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Calculation of the D/E ratio
- Interpretation of the implications for the cost of capital and riskiness
- Monitoring of the financing policies between debt and own capital
