# Lessons 2.6-2.7: Financial Ratios and Financial Leverage

## Topic Overview

ROE, ROI and financial leverage. The lessons present the financial ratios used to assess profitability, solidity and liquidity. They go in depth into ROE, ROI, the cost of debt (R), the S factor, the leverage ratio D/E and the relationship ROE = ROI + (D/E) × (ROI − R) × S. They also introduce ROT, solidity ratios and liquidity indicators, explaining how to link the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement to interpret profitability, financial structure and the sustainability of cash flows.

---

## Key Points Summary

1. Introduction to financial ratios for a synthetic reading of the company's profitability, liquidity and equity solidity
2. Link between the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement to understand the economic result, the financial structure and the cash flows
3. Objective of the profitability ratios: verify the economic equilibrium and measure profitability for shareholders and its determinants
4. ROE (Return on Equity): ratio between net profit for the year and equity; measures profitability for shareholders
5. ROI (Return on Investment): ratio between operating profit and invested capital; measures the profitability of the operating activity relative to the resources used
6. R index (average cost of third-party capital): (financial expenses − financial income) / third-party funds (TPF); average cost of debt net of financial income
7. S index: net profit for the year / profit before tax from continued operations; captures the effect of taxes and discontinued operations on profitability
8. Leverage ratio (debt ratio) D/E or TPF/E: third-party funds / equity; measures the degree of indebtedness relative to own capital
9. Financial leverage relationship linking ROE, ROI, R, S and D/E: ROE = ROI + (D/E) × (ROI − R) × S (conceptual formula as described in the lesson)
10. Condition for the convenience of debt: if ROI > R, an increase in the leverage ratio raises profitability for shareholders

**Key Quote:**
> "If the company is able to make debt capital earn more than it costs, debt becomes a lever that increases profitability for shareholders." (Lesson on financial ratios)

## Overview of the Financial Statement Documents and Purpose of the Ratios

The lesson links the three dimensions of company performance to the main financial statement documents: the income statement (economic result), the balance sheet (structure of uses and sources of funds), the cash flow statement (ability to generate liquidity). Financial ratios allow a synthetic and schematic reading of profitability, liquidity and equity solidity, going beyond the mere analytical examination of the individual items.

**Key Points:**
- **Income statement:** measures the economic result (operating profit, net profit)
- **Balance sheet:** shows invested capital, equity, third-party funds
- **Cash flow statement:** shows cash flows from operating activity and other areas
- **Financial ratios:** synthetic tools for profitability, liquidity, solidity
- The current lesson deals with the profitability ratios; the next one deals with solidity and liquidity

**Explanation:**
Financial statement analysts have developed ratios to quickly obtain an overall view of profitability, liquidity and solidity. The lesson explains how each financial statement document provides a piece and how the ratios combine this information into aggregate and interpretable measures.

**Considerations:**
- Use the correct items of the income statement and balance sheet for the numerators and denominators of the ratios
- Integrate the information from the cash flow statement for the medium-to-long-term ratios

## ROE (Return on Equity)

ROE is defined as net profit for the year / equity. It measures profitability for shareholders, that is, the company's ability to generate an economic result intended to remunerate own capital.

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** net profit for the year (last line of the income statement)
- **Denominator:** equity (liabilities and equity side of the balance sheet)
- Indicates the remuneration of the shareholders' capital
- It is influenced by operating, financial and tax determinants

**Explanation:**
To interpret ROE correctly, it is broken down into its determinants: operating profitability (ROI), cost of debt (R), financial leverage (D/E) and taxes/discontinued operations (S). In this way it is possible to understand which levers are generating the result for the shareholders.

**Considerations:**
- Compare ROE with the cost of own capital to assess value creation
- Analyze the sustainability of the ROE level relative to risk and leverage

---

## ROI (Return on Investment)

ROI is defined as operating profit / invested capital. It measures the contribution of the operating activity to the overall profitability relative to the total resources used (own and third-party capital).

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** operating profit (the first intermediate balance in the income statement)
- **Denominator:** invested capital (overall value of the resources; coincides with total assets, i.e. D + E)
- Indicates how much the core activity yields per unit of capital employed

**Explanation:**
By calculating ROI one observes the operating efficiency in transforming resources into operating result. It is a basis for comparing with the cost of third-party capital (R) and understanding the convenience of debt.

**Considerations:**
- Define the invested capital (D + E) consistently with the perimeter of the operating profit
- Use data that is homogeneous for the period to avoid distortions

---

## R Index (Average Cost of Third-Party Capital)

R measures the average cost of debt: (financial expenses − financial income) / third-party funds (TPF). It is calculated using the items below the net operating margin line in the income statement, and at the denominator the sum of current and non-current liabilities.

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** financial expenses − financial income (interest expense net of interest income)
- **Denominator:** third-party funds (TPF), total debts = current liabilities + non-current liabilities
- Interpretable as the average interest paid on the borrowed capital

**Explanation:**
The cost of debt is cleaned of the effect of financial income, obtaining a net cost. This allows comparison with ROI to assess whether debt creates or destroys value.

**Considerations:**
- Use time consistency between numerator and denominator (same financial year)
- Pay attention to extraordinary components in financial income/expenses

---

## S Index (Taxes and Discontinued Operations)

S = net profit for the year / profit before tax from continued operations. It aggregates the effect of taxes and discontinued operations on overall profitability.

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** net profit for the year (last line of the income statement)
- **Denominator:** profit before tax from continued operations (penultimate line, before the tax management and the discontinued/held-for-disposal activities)
- Captures the overall impact of taxation and discontinued operations

**Explanation:**
S dampens or amplifies the effect of the operating and financial determinants on the final profitability for shareholders. In the leverage relationship, S multiplies the entire expression, affecting ROE.

**Considerations:**
- Verify the presence and size of discontinued operations and of the effective tax rate
- Separate non-recurring effects for a cleaner analysis

## Leverage Ratio (D/E or TPF/E)

Debt ratio defined as third-party funds / equity (D/E or TPF/E). It measures the overall degree of indebtedness relative to own capital.

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** third-party funds (total debts)
- **Denominator:** equity
- A higher D/E implies greater leverage and riskiness, with a potential amplification of profitability for shareholders if ROI > R

**Explanation:**
Financial leverage amplifies the effect of the difference between ROI and R on ROE. If ROI exceeds R, an increase in D/E increases ROE; conversely, if ROI < R, leverage reduces ROE.

**Considerations:**
- Assess the sustainability of leverage relative to covenants and the volatility of flows
- Monitor the cost of debt and the maturities to avoid liquidity strains

## Financial Leverage Relationship (Linking ROE, ROI, R, S, D/E)

Mathematical relationship that shows the link between operating profitability, cost of debt, leverage and taxes: ROE is a function of ROI, of the differential (ROI − R), of the leverage ratio (D/E) and of the factor S. The relationship, as formulated in the lesson, is: ROE = ROI + (D/E) × (ROI − R) × S.

**Key Points:**
- If ROI − R > 0, debt increases ROE in proportion to D/E and to S
- S dampens or amplifies the entire relationship (tax effect and discontinued operations)
- Financial management (R) is crucial to transform operating profitability into profitability for shareholders

**Explanation:**
The differential between ROI and R is analyzed: when the company earns more operationally than the cost of debt, using third-party capital is advantageous. The size of the benefit for shareholders depends on the level of leverage and on the tax-operating factor S.

**Example:**
Assume a company that borrows money at 3% (R = 3%) and generates an operating profitability of 4% (ROI = 4%). In this condition, each euro invested earns 4%, while each euro of debt costs 3%. Financial leverage (D/E) increases ROE because the differential (ROI − R) = 1% is positive. The overall effect on ROE is further modulated by S (which reflects taxes and discontinued operations).

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify ROI = 4% and R = 3%
- Calculate the differential: ROI − R = 1%
- Observe that D/E > 0 amplifies the differential in favor of the shareholders
- Consider S as a multiplier of the entire relationship, reducing or increasing the net effect depending on taxation and discontinued operations

**Considerations:**
- Periodically verify the ROI − R differential and the level of D/E
- Include S in the analysis to avoid overestimating the expected ROE

## ROT (Asset Turnover)

ROT = revenues / invested capital. It measures how many times the resources available to the company "turn over" to generate the revenues of the year. It indicates efficiency in the use of assets in the short term.

**Key Points:**
- **Numerator:** revenues of the year
- **Denominator:** invested capital
- **Interpretation:** number of "turns" of the capital to obtain the revenues

**Explanation:**
A higher ROT suggests a greater ability to use capital efficiently to generate sales. Short-term variations reflect choices on the use of resources and market dynamics.

**Example:**
Total invested capital = 100; revenues = 200; ROT = 2. It is as if the company had to use twice the total amount of invested capital to generate that level of revenues.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify invested capital = 100
- Identify revenues = 200
- Calculate ROT = 200 / 100 = 2
- Interpret: two "turns" of the capital to generate the revenues of the year

**Considerations:**
- Compare ROT over time and with peers to assess commercial and operating efficiency
- Avoid isolated interpretations: cross-check with margins to understand the trade-off between volume and profitability

## Equity Solidity Ratios

They measure the structure of the company's investments and financing: financial autonomy (the share of invested capital belonging to the shareholders) and elasticity of financing (the share that can be renegotiated in the short term).

**Key Points:**
- **Financial autonomy** = equity / invested capital (E / (D + E))
- **Elasticity of financing** = current liabilities / invested capital
- Greater autonomy reduces the constraints from third-party lenders
- Elasticity indicates the portion of liabilities that can be renegotiated within the next financial year

**Explanation:**
A high financial autonomy allows greater strategic decision-making freedom. A high elasticity signals short-term flexibility, but also greater exposure to near-term maturities.

**Considerations:**
- Assess the balance between autonomy and the opportunity cost of own capital
- Proactively manage maturities to optimize elasticity without increasing refinancing risk

## Liquidity Ratios

They verify the financial equilibrium in the short and in the medium-to-long term: the current ratio (current assets / current liabilities) and the medium-to-long-term coverage ratio (operating cash flow / non-current liabilities).

**Key Points:**
- **Current ratio:** ideal threshold = 1; >1 excess liquidity; <1 liquidity deficit
- **Medium-to-long term:** net operating cash flow / non-current liabilities; interpreted as the inverse of the years of cash needed
- Example: long-term debts = 100, operating flow = 50, indicator = 0.5 (inverse of 2 years)

**Explanation:**
In the short term, the aim is a balance between liquidable resources and obligations due within the next financial year. In the medium-to-long term, the sustainability of the obligations beyond the next financial year is assessed on the basis of operating cash generation.

**Example:**
Long-term debts equal to 100; liquidity deriving from continuing operations equal to 50. Indicator = 0.5, interpreted as the inverse of the 2 years needed to cover these debts with the operating cash flow.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify non-current liabilities = 100
- Identify net operating cash flow = 50
- Calculate the indicator = 50 / 100 = 0.5
- Interpret as the inverse of 2 years of cash to cover the long-term debts

**Considerations:**
- If the current ratio > 1, assess more profitable uses of the excess liquidity (e.g. investments in durable assets)
- If the current ratio < 1, analyze the timing of the flows to prevent strains
- Use data from the cash flow statement for the medium-to-long-term assessment

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a short-term liquidity deficit is found, how should it be handled? Analyze the time distribution of the cash flows expected within the next financial year and activate working capital management measures (advances, extension of supplier terms, inventory reduction)
- If the elasticity of financing is low, how should it be handled? Plan refinancing and negotiations on non-current liabilities in advance, exploring committed lines and long-term instruments
