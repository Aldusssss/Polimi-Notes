# Lesson 1.3: Business Strategy and Risk Management

## Topic Overview

Business portfolio, vertical integration and risk management. The lesson deals with corporate strategy: product portfolio, company boundaries and make or buy, technological and organizational setup, brand and communication. It analyzes change and innovation, the nature of risk and the tools to manage it, and the economic value for shareholders and stakeholders. It introduces valuation through the discounting of cash flows and distinguishes the economic and financial perspectives, highlighting flexibility, intellectual property and reputation.

---

## Key Points Summary

1. Business portfolio: focused, related, conglomerate/diversified
2. Company boundaries: activities performed in-house/outside, outsourcing, vertical integration
3. Make or buy: a periodic choice that management can adapt
4. Technological-organizational setup: the technologies adopted and the internal organizational structure
5. Image/brand and communication: corporate image, values, messages and media
6. Legal-financial-ownership setup: legal form, ownership, management (recalled from the previous lesson)
7. Change: external factors (political-social context, consumption habits, technology, knowledge, infrastructure, regulation, critical resources) and their impact on the company
8. Innovation: process and product/service innovation to adapt to and anticipate change
9. Risk: intrinsic to the company, uncertain future outcome, investment as a bet
10. Risk management: operational flexibility, brand/reputation/knowledge, patents, insurance instruments

**Key Quotes:**
> "Change is the normal condition in the life of a company."

> "There is no business without risk."

## Business Portfolio

The set of the company's products and services, which can be classified as focused (one or a few products/services), related (different products but with common characteristics: same target, similar processes), diversified/conglomerate (multiple products that differ from one another in target and processes).

**Key Points:**
- **Focused:** portfolio with one or very few products/services
- **Related:** different products with common elements (target, production processes)
- **Conglomerate/diversified:** multiple products, heterogeneous in target and processes
- Example: phones and tablets as a related portfolio

**Explanation:**
The classification of the portfolio helps to understand the offering strategy and the management of skills and processes. Relatedness allows economies of scope, while diversification broadens market coverage and dilutes specific risk.

**Example:**
A company that makes phones and tablets offers different products that share similar targets and similar technological production processes (components, software, supply chain).

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identification of the target: users of mobile devices
- Production processes: assembly lines and procurement of electronic components
- Synergies: shared software platforms, single brand

**Considerations:**
- Assess the synergies between products to maximize economies of scope
- Avoid excessive concentration on a single product if the market is volatile
- Balance the breadth of the portfolio with organizational and technological capabilities

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the portfolio is excessively focused in a market subject to rapid technological obsolescence, how should it be handled? Gradually diversify towards related products to exploit existing skills

## Company Boundaries and Vertical Integration

The boundaries include activities performed internally and externally. Outsourcing moves production phases outside the company. Vertical integration increases internal activities, including the acquisition of suppliers.

**Key Points:**
- **Outsourcing:** entrusting components, raw materials and semi-finished goods to third parties
- **Vertical integration:** internalization of activities and acquisition of suppliers
- **Trade-off:** decision-making simplicity vs. efficiency of external specialists
- **Make or buy choice:** periodic, not definitive, dependent on context

**Explanation:**
Management constantly evaluates the optimal degree of vertical integration to reduce negotiation complexity and improve control, balancing it against the efficiency and specialization of external suppliers. Make or buy choices are updated according to opportunities and changes in context.

**Example:**
A company buys a supplier of critical components, internalizing procurement and reducing supply risks.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Analysis of the high transaction costs in negotiations
- Benefits of coordination and controlled quality
- Risks: rigidity, specific investments, lower flexibility

**Considerations:**
- Estimate transaction costs and coordination benefits
- Assess the impact on production flexibility
- Analyze the risks of dependence on external suppliers vs. the costs of internalization

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a supplier holds critical know-how and threatens price increases, how should it be handled? Consider vertical integration or long-term agreements with price and quality clauses

## Technological-Organizational Setup

The set of technologies used and the organizational structure (units, responsibilities). Advanced technologies enable continuous innovation; the organization defines roles and responsibilities.

**Key Points:**
- Procurement and use of advanced technologies
- Internal structure: organizational units and managers
- Link between technology and innovation capability

**Explanation:**
Observing the company means assessing both its technological endowment (up-to-dateness, compatibility, scalability) and its organizational architecture (clarity of roles, coordination). A later video lesson will explore organizational design in more depth.

**Considerations:**
- Ensure continuous technological updating
- Define clear responsibilities in the organizational units
- Align technology and operating processes

## Image, Brand and Communication

Corporate image/brand as external representation (trademark, logo, values, messages) and systematic communication to customers and the public through media and other channels.

**Key Points:**
- Brand as a synthesis of identity, values and promises
- Consistent and systematic communication across media and channels
- Reputation as an asset for risk management

**Explanation:**
Image is not only aesthetics: it embodies ideals and messages that shape the perception of customers, competitors and suppliers. Communication turns the brand into continuing relationships and trust.

**Considerations:**
- Maintain consistency between declared values and operating practices
- Measure reputation and the impact of campaigns
- Integrate the brand into the risk management strategy

## Change and Innovation

Change is constant: it is driven by politics, society, consumption, technology, knowledge, infrastructure, regulation, and critical resources and skills. The company must innovate processes and products/services to adapt.

**Key Points:**
- **Drivers of change:** political-social context, consumption habits, technological development, knowledge, infrastructure, regulation, resources and skills
- **Process innovation:** changing the way of operating
- **Product/service innovation:** new goods for new needs
- Transitions to new paradigms that make products obsolete

**Explanation:**
To survive and thrive, the company tries to anticipate changes by redefining processes, identifying new needs and developing suitable offerings. Innovation becomes a driver of resilience and competitive advantage.

**Considerations:**
- Systematically monitor technological and regulatory trends
- Develop dynamic capabilities to reconfigure resources
- Plan investments in R&D and skills updating

**Special Circumstances:**
- If an emerging technology makes core products obsolete, how should it be handled? Launch pilot projects, reposition the portfolio and accelerate process and product innovation

## Risk and Risk Management

Risk is intrinsic to business activity because results lie in the future and the context is uncertain. Risk management includes operational flexibility, protection of know-how (patents), enhancement of brand/reputation/knowledge, and insurance instruments.

**Key Points:**
- Risk of misidentifying needs, or of inadequate execution or pricing
- The owners' investment as a bet with a future outcome
- **Flexibility:** modulating production quantities and timing
- **Protection of know-how:** patents
- **Insurance instruments** for cash fluctuations and adverse events

**Explanation:**
Since the future is uncertain, the company adopts flexible organizational practices, builds intangible assets (brand, reputation, skills) and protects innovations. As a last resort it uses insurance to stabilize the impact of events. Management defines and updates these tools because risk cannot be eliminated.

**Considerations:**
- Design processes that are elastic with respect to demand
- Manage the intellectual property portfolio
- Integrate insurance with operational prevention tools

**Special Circumstances:**
- If demand fluctuates unpredictably, how should it be handled? Implement scalable production capacity and plans to modulate cash outflows

## Economic Value of the Company (Shareholders' Perspective)

A company has value for shareholders if it is believed that in the future it will generate net cash flows to them that exceed the flows required from them (acquisition, launch of new activities, conversions, covering losses).

**Key Points:**
- Relevance of future activities as a source of value
- Subjectivity of the valuation: it depends on the eyes of the evaluator
- **Positive flows for shareholders:** dividends and return of capital
- **Negative flows for shareholders:** paid-in capital increases

**Explanation:**
The definition favors the shareholders' point of view and requires informed estimates of future flows. The central idea is that value arises if the outflows required from shareholders are lower than the net inflows the company will return in the future.

**Considerations:**
- Make explicit the assumptions on growth, margins and dividend policy
- Assess future capital needs and operating risks
- Recognize the variability of the estimate across different evaluators

## Analytical Valuation: Discounting of Cash Flows

The economic value at time zero (V0) is the sum of the net cash flows at each time t, discounted with the coefficient (1+K). The time horizon is potentially infinite; the version with a large T includes a terminal value at time T.

**Key Points:**
- V0 as the sum of discounted net cash flows
- **Discounting:** making future flows comparable at time zero using (1+K)
- Infinite horizon vs. finite horizon with terminal value
- **Components of the flows for shareholders:** dividends (+), return of capital (+), paid-in capital increases (-)

**Explanation:**
Discounting removes the effect of the loss of value of money over time. The version with a large T sums the flows up to T and adds the terminal value, which represents the company's future ability, beyond T, to generate value for the owners.

**Considerations:**
- Determine a K appropriate to the risk and to the cost of capital
- Define the terminal value consistently (perpetual growth method or multiples)
- Allocate the signs of the flows correctly according to the perspective adopted

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the flows are highly volatile and uncertain beyond a certain horizon, how should it be handled? Limit T to reasonable visibility and use scenarios for the terminal value

## Economic Result: Economic and Financial Perspectives

The economic result is the difference between the value of output and the value of inputs at a time t. Financial perspective: cash inflows from sales minus cash outflows for the acquisition of inputs.

**Key Points:**
- **Output:** goods and services sold
- **Input:** labor, technologies, infrastructure, materials
- The sum of the results year after year gives the overall economic value

**Explanation:**
Value can be observed through economic measures (value of exchanges) or financial ones (cash flows). Both converge in the assessment of the annual contribution to the overall value.

**Considerations:**
- Consistency between economic measures and cash flows
- Reconcile economic and financial accounting
- Measure inputs and outputs correctly in value terms

## Extended Economic Value: Stakeholders and Society

Beyond the value for shareholders, the company generates value for stakeholders (workers, suppliers, customers) and for society, through employment, quality relationships, prosperity of supply chains, benefits for infrastructure and economic and social growth.

**Key Points:**
- Positive relationships with workers and suppliers as part of the value
- Impact on workers' families: security and stability
- **Customers:** satisfaction of needs through goods/services
- **Indirect benefits:** infrastructure, economic growth, social opportunities

**Explanation:**
Recent thinking broadens the concept of value to include the external and relational effects of business activity. Extended value embraces the sustainability of relationships and the systemic impact on the market and on society.

**Considerations:**
- Map stakeholders and non-financial value metrics
- Integrate ESG objectives with economic performance
- Communicate extended value transparently
