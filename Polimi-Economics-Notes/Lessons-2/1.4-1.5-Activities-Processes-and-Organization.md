# Lessons 1.4-1.5: Activities, Processes and Organizational Structure

## Topic Overview

Value chain, business processes, functional/divisional organization. The lessons introduce the definition and mapping of business activities, the distinction between primary and support activities in the value chain, and the criteria for classification and synthesis (ABC curve). They go into processes vs. projects, efficiency/effectiveness KPIs, the role of the process owner, SCM and make-or-buy. They conclude with organizational design (functional/divisional), trade-offs and integration mechanisms, highlighting how to adapt structure and governance to the competitive context.

---

## Key Points Summary

1. Definition of a business activity as a homogeneous set of operations within the company
2. Relationship between activities and business processes: activities combine into sequences that generate processes
3. Different representations of activities depending on the level of disaggregation/aggregation
4. Value chain: primary activities and support activities
5. Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, after-sales services
6. Support activities: infrastructure, human resource management, technology development/R&D, procurement
7. No general rules on the number and type of activities: it depends on managerial criteria
8. Criteria for classifying activities: homogeneity of operations, associated resources, competitive impact
9. Logical scheme for identifying activities: mapping, ABC curve, analysis of skills/drivers, grouping, attribution of secondary activities
10. ABC curve to identify main activities (80% of costs) vs. secondary ones

**Key Quotes:**
> "Improving efficiency and effectiveness does not come from a generic attempt to improve everything, but from a careful analysis of the performance of individual activities."

> "The choice of organizational structure is never by the book: it must reflect the specific characteristics of the company and of the context in which it operates."

> "The organizational structure serves to put things in order, but it must not be considered a rigid element: it can and must be made flexible in relation to needs, processes and projects." (Lecture of 2025-09-24)

## Business Activities: Definition and Levels of Representation

Business activities are homogeneous sets of operations carried out within the company that make it possible to transform inputs (human, technological, financial resources) into outputs (goods and services). The representation of activities depends on the level of disaggregation chosen by management, producing different maps even for similar companies.

**Key Points:**
- Activities as the basic unit of the input-output transformation
- Homogeneity of operations as the defining criterion
- Differences in representation linked to the level of analytical detail
- The activity map as a management tool

**Explanation:**
In the course, the company is seen as a transformation system. Identifying activities means making explicit "what is done" to carry out the transformation. Depending on the degree of detail (macro vs. micro), the same company can appear with very different activity maps. Management chooses the level that is useful for its analysis and control purposes.

**Considerations:**
- Define in advance the level of detail needed for the managerial objectives
- Ensure internal consistency in the classification of activities
- Avoid duplications or overlaps of similar activities

**Special Circumstances:**
- If different units describe activities at different levels of detail, how are they harmonized? Align a glossary and common criteria before the mapping

## Value Chain: Primary and Support Activities

A synthetic structure that groups business activities into primary (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, after-sales services) and support (infrastructure, human resource management, technology development/R&D, procurement).

**Key Points:**
- Primary activities are directly tied to the production and delivery of goods/services
- Support activities enable and sustain the primary ones
- Explicit examples of each category

**Explanation:**
Primary activities cover the end-to-end flow: resources coming in (inbound logistics), transformation (operations), outflow (outbound logistics), the market (marketing and sales), after-sales services. Support activities include physical/IT infrastructure, HR (recruitment, promotion, roles), R&D/process and product innovation, procurement of resources and raw materials.

**Considerations:**
- Clarity in the boundary between primary and support activities
- Align R&D with the needs of the primary processes
- Integrate procurement with logistics and operations

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a support activity becomes core (e.g. a technology platform), how should it be treated? Consider reclassifying it as primary if it directly affects the output for the customer

## Criteria for Mapping and Classifying Activities

There are no universal rules on how many and which activities to identify. The key criteria are: homogeneity of operations, resources associated with each activity, competitive impact.

**Key Points:**
- **Homogeneity:** group similar operations in the same activity
- **Associated resources:** how many and which resources each activity consumes
- **Competitive impact:** relevance of the activity in determining the offering and the competitive advantage

**Explanation:**
Management uses the three criteria to build a structured map of the set of activities. Balancing homogeneity and relevance avoids excessive fragmentation or excessive aggregation.

**Considerations:**
- Document the assumptions used in the classification
- Involve function managers for validation
- Periodically update the criteria according to the strategy

**Special Circumstances:**
- If an activity is heterogeneous but has a strong competitive impact, how should it be treated? Keep it separate for dedicated analysis despite the heterogeneity

## Logical Scheme for Identifying and Synthesizing Activities

Typical sequence: mapping of activities; construction of the ABC curve; analysis of the skills and drivers of the main activities; grouping of the main activities; attribution of the secondary activities to the main ones.

**Key Points:**
- Exhaustive initial mapping
- ABC curve to weigh resource/cost consumption
- Analysis of skills and drivers to understand what enables performance
- Grouping to avoid excessive granularity
- Attribution of secondary activities to main clusters

**Explanation:**
It starts from a complete inventory. The ABC curve ranks activities by resource consumption. Key skills and enabling factors are identified. Complexity is reduced by grouping, and secondary activities are linked back to main macro-activities for focused management.

**Considerations:**
- Ensure traceability between the initial map and the final clusters
- Define transparent grouping criteria
- Validate the drivers with data and operational insight

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the grouping hides local issues, how should it be managed? Keep secondary KPIs for granular monitoring even inside the clusters

## ABC Curve to Identify Main and Secondary Activities

A tool whose vertical axis is the percentage of cumulative total costs and whose horizontal axis is the set of activities ordered by resources consumed. The main activities explain eighty percent of total costs; the rest are secondary.

**Key Points:**
- Ordering of activities by resource/cost consumption
- Operational definition: main = up to eighty percent of cumulative costs
- The number of main activities depends on the concentration of costs
- Sensitivity to the way activities are grouped

**Explanation:**
In companies with high costs concentrated in a few activities, the main set will be small; in contexts with widespread costs, the number of main activities will be larger. The ABC curve guides priorities for analysis and improvement.

**Considerations:**
- Check the quality of the cost data
- Explore different granularities to test the stability of the classification
- Avoid distortions caused by non-robust indirect allocations

**Special Circumstances:**
- If detailed cost data is missing, how should it proceed? Use resource proxies (hours, volumes, capacity) and refine iteratively

## Managerial Focus on Efficiency, Effectiveness and Value

Management looks for the optimal mix of activities and concentrates attention on those that add value, reducing or cutting non-value-adding activities to improve overall performance and the creation of economic value.

**Key Points:**
- Careful analysis of performance by activity
- The activity portfolio as an object of continuous review
- Identification and reduction of non-value-adding activities

**Explanation:**
Improvement is not generic and holistic but selective and evidence-based. A pipeline of initiatives is built on relevant activities and the weight of non-value-adding ones is contained, affecting costs and output quality.

**Considerations:**
- Define clear criteria for "value added"
- Monitor the side effects of cuts on quality and risk
- Align the choices with the competitive strategy

**Special Circumstances:**
- If an activity seems non-value-adding but is required for compliance, how should it be treated? Optimize and automate it, do not cut it; keep the minimum necessary control

## Classification of Activities: Ordinary vs. Project-Based

Activities can be continuous, carried out steadily in constant ways, or project-based, tied to specific non-continuous projects with specific objectives and a temporary nature.

**Key Points:**
- **Ordinary:** repetitive, standardized, stable over time
- **Project-based:** unique, temporary, explicit objectives, dedicated resources

**Explanation:**
The development of a new product is typically project-based; current production is ordinary. The nature affects governance, KPIs and resources.

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the start/end of project-based activities
- Manage resource allocation between ordinary work and projects
- Set KPIs consistent with the nature of the activity

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a project becomes recurring, how should it be treated? Standardize it and move it into the perimeter of ordinary activities with flow processes

## Measuring the Performance of Activities

Performance can be measured with qualitative indicators (effectiveness: response times, satisfaction, quality) and quantitative ones (efficiency: volumes, number of operations, costs, resources consumed).

**Key Points:**
- Qualitative KPIs oriented towards effectiveness
- Quantitative KPIs oriented towards efficiency
- Use of the results to plan improvement actions at the level of activity and organizational unit

**Explanation:**
Middle management, having visibility on results by activity, can define targeted action plans to improve local and systemic performance.

**Considerations:**
- Select a few relevant KPIs to avoid overload
- Ensure measures that are comparable over time
- Link KPIs to clear responsibilities

**Special Circumstances:**
- If an activity has an output that is hard to measure, how should it be evaluated? Use outcome proxies and structured internal/external customer feedback

## Management Levers for Improvement

Three typical approaches: reorganization of individuals (number and skills), reorganization of processes (recombination of activities), rearticulation of organizational units.

**Key Points:**
- Sizing and skill mix of staff
- Redesign of processes and activity sequences
- Redesign of the formal organization to enable efficiency/effectiveness

**Explanation:**
The choice of levers depends on the diagnosis of root causes. Interventions are often combined and iterative, with post-implementation monitoring.

**Considerations:**
- Assess the impacts on change management
- Align incentives and roles with the new configuration
- Plan transitions with risks and mitigations

**Special Circumstances:**
- If union constraints limit the reorganization, how should it proceed? Focus on processes and technology, negotiate gradual changes and training

## Business Processes: Definition and Centrality

A business process is a set of activities aimed at creating an output that is relevant for an internal or external customer. It is the preferred unit of analysis because its performance can be measured through the overall output and the links between activities influence the result.

**Key Points:**
- Output as the measure of process performance
- Internal/external customer as the reference for value
- Interdependencies between activities determine overall effectiveness and efficiency

**Explanation:**
Attention shifts from the micro level (activity) to the meso level (process) to capture the effects of coordination, waiting times and handovers that affect the final output.

**Example:**
Sequence of activities: taking part in the tender to win the project; defining the detailed project; operations to carry out the consulting work; drafting the final report. The link between these activities affects the quality, timing and cost of the final report.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the key activities of the process
- Map the interdependencies and information handovers
- Define output KPIs (report quality, delivery times, customer satisfaction) and process KPIs (lead time between phases, rework rate)

**Considerations:**
- Clarify the objective and expected output of each process
- Align end-to-end responsibilities to avoid silos
- Manage the interfaces between activities to reduce waiting and errors

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the customer is internal, how can sub-optimization be avoided? Define internal SLAs and link KPIs to the outcomes of the primary processes

## Types of Processes: Primary vs. Support

Primary processes generate value for the final customer (goods/services); support processes produce intermediate outputs for other internal organizational units, necessary to sustain the primary processes.

**Key Points:**
- Nature of the customer as the distinguishing criterion
- Support processes include HR, procurement, etc.
- Interdependence: support processes enable the primary ones

**Explanation:**
The taxonomy guides the priority of investments and the definition of KPIs: external for the primary processes, internal for the support ones, aligned with the final outcomes.

**Considerations:**
- Prevent support processes from optimizing locally at the expense of the primary ones
- Establish internal service agreements
- Monitor the impact of support processes on external KPIs

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a support process directly affects the customer (e.g. the IT of a digital service), how should it be measured? Add external KPIs (uptime, NPS)

## Repetitive/Flow Processes vs. Impulse Processes/Projects

Repetitive processes are standardized and permanent, with sequential interdependencies typical of the supplier-customer chain; impulse processes (projects) are discontinuous, with a unique output and process, temporary, with dedicated resources and reciprocal interdependencies.

**Key Points:**
- **Repetitive:** ordinary operation, codified procedures, continuous flow
- **Projects:** explicit objective, temporary nature, new product development as an example
- Differences in interdependencies: sequential vs. reciprocal

**Explanation:**
The nature of the process defines the governance, planning, control and capabilities needed. Projects require project management, flows require process management and continuous improvement.

**Example:**
Purchase of raw materials from the supplier; transformation operations into products/services; sales and transfer to the external customer. Each phase feeds the next in a sequential chain.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Define input/output for each phase
- Map throughput times and bottlenecks
- Align capacity between phases to avoid accumulations

**Considerations:**
- Standardize flow processes with stable procedures and KPIs
- Manage projects with plans, milestones and dedicated governance
- Adapt resources to the nature of the process

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a flow process shows high variability, how should it be managed? Introduce buffers, cross-training, and statistical improvement of the variability

## Components of the Process Description: Flow, Boundaries, Responsibilities

To analyze a process, the flow of activities (interdependencies and sequences), the boundaries of the process (which activities to include) and the organizational responsibilities are defined.

**Key Points:**
- **Flow:** representation of the phases and the links
- **Boundaries:** perimeter of the process, activities included/excluded
- **Responsibilities:** who is accountable for the end-to-end result

**Explanation:**
Changing the boundaries changes the representation and the management. Clear responsibilities avoid a "no man's land" between functions and improve performance on the output.

**Considerations:**
- Clearly document the start/end of the process
- Assign a process owner with adequate authority
- Update the boundaries if products/markets change

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the process crosses several functions, how can accountability be ensured? Establish cross-functional governance and shared KPIs

## Process Boundaries and Outsourcing/Insourcing

The boundary of the process defines which activities fall within a business process, even if they are carried out outside the company (outsourcing). Outsourcing means managing the impact of the suppliers' performance on the process and evaluating its economic convenience compared with insourcing.

**Key Points:**
- Process activities can be carried out outside and still be part of the process
- Outsourcing vs. insourcing as strategic-operational choices
- Economic evaluation of convenience (make-or-buy)
- Impact of supplier performance on the times, costs, quality and output of the process

**Explanation:**
It starts from identifying the activities that make up the process and checking where they are carried out. If some phases are external, the effect on overall performance and costs must be analyzed, comparing insourcing alternatives. Management requires coordination with suppliers to keep times and operating methods consistent.

**Example:**
The company buys materials made by another company. Although the activity takes place outside the company boundaries, it remains part of the business process. Delivery times, quality, prices and supply conditions must be monitored so as not to compromise the production flow.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map the process and include the procurement phase
- Define KPIs on supplier times, quality and costs
- Compare the costs/benefits of insourcing the production of the raw materials
- Establish contractual SLAs to align performance

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define what is part of the process even if external
- Establish KPIs and SLAs with suppliers to ensure alignment on times and quality
- Periodically compare make-or-buy based on costs, risks and skills

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the supplier's performance degrades and affects lead time, how should it be handled? Activate improvement plans, provide for alternative suppliers and evaluate partial insourcing

## Supply Chain Management (SCM)

Function that manages the supply chain to align timing, methods and management of the process between different companies, integrating external and internal activities in terms of times, prices, product conditions.

**Key Points:**
- Analysis of the requirements of the relationship with other companies
- Alignment of timing and operating methods in the chain
- Management of purchase prices, conditions and quality
- Integration of activities carried out by different companies

**Explanation:**
SCM structures the contractual and operational relationships with suppliers to synchronize procurement with production, defining schedules, service levels, quality standards and prices, and reducing variability and bottlenecks.

**Example:**
To start its production process, a company sources raw materials from another company. SCM coordinates delivery times, prices, technical specifications and supply conditions to ensure production continuity.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Definition of shared forecasts and MRP plans
- Agreements on lead time, minimum lots and stock levels
- Monitoring of OTIF and quality at acceptance
- Management of exceptions and contingency plans

**Considerations:**
- Establish metrics such as OTIF, lead time, defect rate at acceptance
- Integrate planning (forecasts, MRP) with critical suppliers
- Provide clauses for changes in price and quality

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the supplier changes the product specifications, how should it be handled? Require formal technical approval, repeat the qualifications and update documentation and contracts

## Role and Responsibilities of the Process Owner

The process owner is responsible for the execution and overall results of the process, for the relationship with the customer (internal or external), and for continuous improvement in times, methods, costs and performance. They may have no hierarchical authority over the participants in the process.

**Key Points:**
- Responsibility for the overall result and measurable outputs
- Management of the relationship with the internal/external customer
- Oversight of continuous improvement (times, costs, performance)
- Possible lack of direct hierarchical authority
- Central role in incentive and performance measurement systems

**Explanation:**
The process owner governs the end-to-end flow, negotiates objectives and standards, coordinates actors from different units, defines KPIs and improvement plans. Even without hierarchical authority, they exert influence through governance and metrics.

**Example:**
In an organizational unit with two processes and people reporting to different managers, the process owner is responsible for all the activities of the process but is not the hierarchical head of everyone. They are nonetheless the guarantor of the functioning and the results.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Define RACI and cross-functional KPIs
- Set up periodic governance meetings
- Align incentive systems with process performance
- Escalate to top/middle management in case of blockages

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the purpose, output and KPIs of the process
- Establish cross-cutting governance mechanisms
- Align incentives with the achievement of process results

**Special Circumstances:**
- If functional managers obstruct process changes, how should it be handled? Use top management sponsors, performance data and escalation mechanisms

## Projects (Discontinuous Processes) and Project Management

Projects are characterized by 4 macro-phases: conception (idea and objectives), definition (detailed planning of phases/activities), implementation (operations of the project), closure (analysis of results and possible transformation into a repetitive process). The project manager has cross-cutting responsibility, with specific planning and control (budget, resources, time, monitoring of actuals).

**Key Points:**
- 4 phases: conception, definition, implementation, closure
- Definition of budget, resources and times in the definition phase
- Monitoring and control: analysis of actual times and costs phase by phase
- Transformation of the prototype/new product from project to flow process
- Cross-cutting responsibility of the project manager over the activities and processes involved

**Explanation:**
The project life cycle starts from ideation and objectives, moves to the detailed planning of activities, resources and costs, proceeds with operational execution and ends with closure, including the evaluation of results and standardization for the possible transition to a process.

**Example:**
Conception: define how the product should be made, the idea and the objectives. Definition: plan the activities to make that specific product/service. Implementation: carry out the activities to turn the idea into the final product. Closure: analyze the result and turn the prototype into a repetitive flow process.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Establish measurable objectives and success criteria
- Create a dedicated WBS, schedule and budget
- Perform time/cost control by phase with variance analysis
- Standardize and transfer to the operating process in case of success

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define budget, times and resources in the definition phase
- Systematically measure actual times and costs by phase
- Plan industrialization if the results meet the criteria

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the times of a phase exceed the plan, how should it be handled? Activate variance analysis, reassign critical resources and re-plan the dependencies

## Organization Chart and Hierarchical Levels

The organization chart graphically represents the organization: rectangles for organizational units, horizontal lines for hierarchical levels. More levels indicate a greater hierarchy; a flatter representation indicates fewer levels.

**Key Points:**
- **Organizational unit:** a group of people with a manager who carries out activities
- Typical example: top management at the top (board of directors), below it production and sales; under sales: a unit for small customers and one for large customers
- Possible presence of a general manager between the board of directors and the unit managers
- A CEO may be at the top; CEO (strategy) and general manager (management) can coexist

**Explanation:**
The graphical structure helps to understand reporting lines and responsibilities. The layering indicates the degree of hierarchy and the distribution of decision-making power.

**Example:**
Under the sales unit there are two units: small customers and large customers. Both report to the sales manager, while production and sales report to the board of directors or to a general manager.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Definition of the reporting lines
- Clarity of the markets served (segmentation by customer)
- Alignment of the unit objectives with the company strategy

**Considerations:**
- Keep the chain of command and responsibilities clear
- Assess whether to introduce a general manager depending on complexity
- Align the structure with the markets and products served

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the organization chart becomes too hierarchical, how should it be handled? Consider reducing levels and shortening decision lines to increase agility

## Hierarchical Levels and Staff Functions

Three macro levels: strategic apex (top management: board of directors, general manager, first-line managers), middle management (managers of secondary units), operating core (employees). Staff functions support the primary processes and report directly to the apex, not to the operating lines.

**Key Points:**
- **Top management:** strategic decisions and top-level responsibilities
- **Middle management:** responsibility for secondary units reporting to the top
- **Operating core:** execution of the operating work
- **Staff functions:** management control, HR, administration and finance, legal affairs
- Staff as cross-cutting functions not hierarchically linked to production

**Explanation:**
The distinction clarifies decision-making and operating roles. Staff functions provide cross-cutting specialist support, improving control, compliance and resource allocation.

**Example:**
Cost analysis and management control report to the strategic apex (e.g. the general manager), not to the production managers, to guarantee independence and a cross-cutting view.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Definition of independent reporting and KPIs
- Support for strategic decisions with reliable data

**Considerations:**
- Preserve the independence of the staff functions
- Ensure effective communication channels between staff and line
- Align staff objectives with the company strategy

**Special Circumstances:**
- If staff is perceived as an operating obstacle, how should it be handled? Define internal SLAs and coordination moments to improve service to the line units

## Designing the Organizational Structure: Grouping Activities and Building the Hierarchy

Two key decisions: 1) how to group activities into organizational units (which and how many activities per unit), 2) how to group elementary units into higher-level units (building the hierarchy). The choice depends on the characteristics, resources, people, stage of development and markets of the company.

**Key Points:**
- Decisions on the content of the units and their hierarchical aggregation
- No single solution: dependence on the specific context
- Adaptation to strategies and available resources

**Explanation:**
Management defines the boundaries and responsibilities of the units, then designs the reporting levels to ensure coordination and control, balancing specialization and integration.

**Example:**
Two companies in the same sector adopt different structures for reasons tied to strategy, resources, people and markets, showing that there is no single model valid for everyone.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Analysis of the internal and external context
- Design consistent with strategic priorities

**Considerations:**
- Avoid standardized approaches that ignore the context
- Align the structure with strategic and operating priorities
- Periodically review the structure as markets evolve

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the company enters a new market, how should it be handled? Consider creating a dedicated division or a geographic unit to maximize focus and accountability

## Grouping Criteria: Input, Output, Geography; Functional and Divisional Structures

Grouping by input: combine activities that use the same resources; by output: combine activities aimed at the same product/customer/market; by location: combine activities at the same site. Functional structure when input prevails; divisional when output or geography prevails.

**Key Points:**
- **Functional:** commonality of resources/inputs
- **Divisional:** focus on product/output or territory
- Production sites as autonomous units for the local production cycle
- Choice dependent on operating consistency and market focus

**Explanation:**
The grouping logic drives specialization (functional) or focus on product/market (divisional). Geography can require autonomy for operating and local-context reasons.

**Example:**
A department that uses different inputs for a specific output is divisional; a department that shares the same inputs/skills is functional.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map inputs/outputs to define the dominant logic
- Assess the impacts on coordination and time-to-market

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the dominant logic (input/output/geography)
- Assess the trade-off between functional efficiencies and customer/product orientation
- Establish interfaces between functions and divisions to reduce silos

**Special Circumstances:**
- If products and markets evolve rapidly, how should it be handled? Favor divisional or hybrid structures to increase responsiveness

## Interdependencies Between Activities and Their Impact on the Organization

The relationships between activities influence the design of the units: process interdependencies (sequentiality), skill interdependencies (shared or complementary skills), interdependencies in the use of resources (economies of scale), socio-cultural interdependencies (local contexts and traditions).

**Key Points:**
- Process interdependencies suggest units that follow the operating sequence
- Skill interdependencies suggest units that concentrate consistent know-how
- Interdependencies in the use of resources enable economies of scale and lower unit costs
- Socio-cultural interdependencies explain units by plant/territory with shared practices

**Explanation:**
Identifying the links between activities makes it possible to design units that maximize coordination, learning and efficiency, reducing costs and cultural friction.

**Example:**
The procurement of raw materials and their initial storage are sequential phases: organizing them in the same unit can reduce throughput times and handover errors.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map the value chain
- Co-locate sequential activities or create integrated teams

**Considerations:**
- Map interdependencies before defining the unit boundaries
- Exploit economies of scale by sharing critical resources
- Consider local cultural factors in foreign sites or districts

**Special Circumstances:**
- If teams with strong cultural differences need to be integrated, how should it be handled? Introduce cultural integration programs and local bridging leadership

## Economies of Scale and Scale Effects

Inverse relationship between the quantities produced and the unit cost when fixed costs prevail. Spreading the fixed cost over a larger number of units reduces the unit incidence of the cost and suggests creating organizational units that synergistically exploit shared resources.

**Key Points:**
- The unit production cost decreases as the quantities produced increase
- Prevalence of fixed costs (e.g. depreciation of machinery) as a typical condition
- Spreading the fixed cost across more units/quantities as the mechanism for reducing the unit cost
- Organization oriented towards the maximum use of shareable resources

**Explanation:**
Assume a production process represented with unit cost on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Some goods/services show an inverse relationship between quantity and unit cost because of the prevalence of fixed costs. Spreading these costs over more units reduces the incidence per unit. It is therefore convenient to create organizational units that can share and exploit these resources, maximizing the use of the fixed cost or of costs that can be shared between different units.

**Example:**
A machine with a fixed annual depreciation cost is used to produce a growing number of pieces. By spreading the fixed cost over a larger number of pieces, the fixed cost per unit falls, making it convenient to concentrate production and organize the units so as to use the machine for higher volumes.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the fixed cost (depreciation)
- Increase the quantities produced at the same fixed cost
- Calculate the unit incidence of the fixed cost (fixed cost/quantity)
- Observe the decrease in unit cost and draw organizational implications to make the most of the resource

**Considerations:**
- Verify the presence of prevailing fixed costs
- Assess the volume thresholds needed to obtain economies of scale
- Avoid diseconomies of scale caused by excessive complexity
- Design units that maximize the use of shared resources

## Functional vs. Divisional Organization Chart

Two ways of structuring the company: functional (units based on resources/inputs) and divisional (units based on output/products or context). The choice depends on competition, product range, market dynamism and company complexity.

**Key Points:**
- **Functional:** based on the resources used (input)
- **Divisional:** based on the output produced or the socio-economic context
- Functional suits low competition, a limited range, a focused portfolio, absence of diseconomies of scale
- Divisional suits dynamic/complex markets and the need to innovate products and operating methods
- Divisional is preferable in large/complex companies to empower units and to set objectives, targets and incentives on output

**Explanation:**
Functional organization charts group activities by type of resource or function (e.g. production, marketing, finance). They are appropriate when there is little competition, the product range is limited and economies of scale are exploited on resources. Divisional organization charts group by output or context (product, market, geographic area) and are preferable in dynamic contexts, when there is a need to innovate, and in large companies, where it is useful to measure performance on outputs, set targets and incentives.

**Example 1 (functional):**
A company with a few standard products, in a stable market, adopts a function-based organization chart (production, purchasing, logistics) to optimize the use of resources and economies of scale.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Analyze competition and product variety
- Group by function to exploit common resources
- Measure efficiency on inputs and unit costs

**Example 2 (divisional):**
A multinational group with constantly evolving products and diversified markets divides its units by product and region, defining objectives and incentives tied to sales, quality and time-to-market.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map product lines and regions
- Assign responsibilities on outputs
- Implement performance and incentive systems for the divisions

**Considerations:**
- Assess market dynamism and the need for innovation
- Measure organizational complexity and company size
- Define performance metrics on outputs when adopting the divisional model
- Ensure that diseconomies of scale are not created in the functional setup

## Divisional Options: By Product, By Market, By Geographic Area

Three main options for divisional structures, with guiding criteria focused on the output or on the reference context: product, market/user target, geographic area.

**Key Points:**
- **By product:** units responsible for specific goods/services
- **By market/target:** units oriented towards types of users/segments, even with the same product but different strategies
- **By geographic area:** units for plants/sites in different countries and areas

**Explanation:**
When the focus is on output, divisions can be created by product line, by markets/customer targets and by geography. The options are not necessarily alternatives and can coexist in complex structures, giving priority to the dominant criterion according to strategy and context.

**Example 1 (by product):**
Product Unit A, Product Unit B, each with end-to-end responsibility for development, production and performance of the good/service.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the product portfolio
- Align resources and processes to the specific line
- Define KPIs on the output of each product

**Example 2 (by market):**
Retail Unit and B2B Unit: they sell the same product but to different customers with different commercial and operating strategies.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Segment customers
- Adapt marketing, sales and service
- Align incentives to results by segment

**Example 3 (by geographic area):**
Europe Unit, Asia Unit, Americas Unit: they manage plants and activities in their respective areas, adapting to local regulations and contexts.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map operating areas and sites
- Adapt processes to local constraints
- Establish regional objectives and accountability

**Considerations:**
- Clarity of the guiding criterion (output/context)
- Compatibility between options and risks of overlap
- Definition of responsibility and measurability for each division
- Adaptation to local regulations and cultures in geographic structures

## Managing Organizational Trade-offs and Integration Mechanisms

Trade-offs emerge between control of inputs (functional) and flexibility on outputs (divisional). Responses include changes over time, hybrid solutions and integration mechanisms such as matrix structures, knowledge management, task forces, committees and integrating managers.

**Key Points:**
- Trade-off between an excessively divisional logic (hard to control inputs) and an excessively functional logic (poor output flexibility)
- Solution: change the setup over time according to market changes
- Solution: hybrid setup (functional in the support activities, divisional in production)
- Integration mechanisms for complex processes: matrix, knowledge management, task forces, committees, integrating managers

**Explanation:**
Management must assess when to favor functional or divisional criteria, recognizing that extremes can generate inefficiencies. Large companies periodically change their structures; they often adopt hybrid models. To integrate activities distributed across functional and divisional units, they use matrix structures with dual reporting, knowledge management systems to map and make skills and activities easy to find, task forces and committees for specific projects, and integrating managers focused on processes/projects who orchestrate skills across units.

**Example 1 (matrix):**
An operating team reports to the product manager for the characteristics of the output and to the functional manager for technical standards, with reporting that differs depending on the phase of the process.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Define dual lines of authority
- Establish priority rules for conflicts
- Monitor performance on output and functional compliance

**Example 2 (knowledge management):**
Creation of maps of the activities and skills in the functional and divisional units, with accessible repositories to start collaborations on specific processes/projects.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Catalog skills and processes
- Implement search and traceability tools
- Define collaboration protocols

**Example 3 (task forces and integrating managers):**
Creation of task forces or committees for specific projects and appointment of integrating managers with no responsibility for units, but with accountability for processes, who gather skills from the different units.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the project/process and the expected results
- Select the necessary skills from the units
- Assign integration responsibilities and timing
- Monitor progress and alignment between functions/divisions

**Considerations:**
- Provide for periodic structural review cycles
- Clearly define responsibilities and reporting in matrix structures
- Create up-to-date repositories for knowledge management
- Establish incentives that are consistent for both input and output
- Manage conflicts between functional and divisional lines with explicit rules

**Special Circumstances:**
- If priority conflicts arise in a matrix structure, how should they be handled? Establish escalation criteria and precedence rules between product managers and functional managers
- If structural rigidity is found that obstructs an urgent project, how should it be handled? Activate temporary task forces and assign an integrating manager to speed up coordination and decisions
- If performance measurement is centered only on inputs, how should it be corrected? Introduce output KPIs for the divisional units and integrate result-based incentive systems
