## Difference Between External and Internal Accounting

Internal accounting, the subject of the third module, is the tool that provides detailed information on the costs and revenues of products, services and organizational units inside the company, differing from external accounting in the classification of costs/revenues and in its purposes.

**Key Points:**
- **Classification of costs and revenues:** in external accounting it is done by nature in the company's general income statement, whereas in internal accounting it is done by function, that is, according to the use of the costs in production
- **Purpose:** external accounting is aimed at communication to the outside (e.g. the financial statements), whereas internal accounting is developed for management needs and to support internal decisions

- Fixed and variable cost
- Product and period cost

## Distinction Between Internal (Management) and External Accounting

Accounting is divided into two main branches with different purposes and characteristics. External accounting, which culminates in the preparation of the financial statements, aims to provide a representation of the company's overall economic, financial and equity situation to external parties (stakeholders), with a primary function of accountability. Its data are aggregated at company level, classified by nature (e.g. personnel cost, depreciation) and provided typically on an annual basis. By contrast, internal (or management) accounting responds to internal management needs, supporting management in its decisions. It provides cost information disaggregated by function (activities, processes, products, cost centers), with timeliness suited to decision-making needs.

**Key Points:**
- **Purpose:** managerial and decision-making for internal accounting; accountability and external representation for external accounting
- **Recipients:** internal management for internal accounting; external stakeholders (investors, banks, etc.) for external accounting
- **Level of detail:** analytical and disaggregated for internal accounting; aggregated and at company level for external accounting
- **Classification of costs:** by function in internal accounting; by nature in external accounting
- **Timeliness:** timely and frequent for internal accounting; periodic (annual) for external accounting

## The Three Main Functions of Internal Accounting

Internal accounting performs three fundamental functions for company management, using detailed cost data that are not available in general accounting.

**Key Points:**
- **Inventory valuation:** it makes it possible to calculate the value of warehouse inventories (unsold finished products, work in progress, raw materials) by assigning them an economic value based on the costs incurred in the specific production activities and processes. This function acts as a bridge between internal and external accounting
- **Management analysis:** it supports the planning, scheduling and control of production. It includes the definition of budgets, the verification of compliance with planned costs and times, and the analysis of variances between forecasts and actual results. This set of activities is known as "management control"
- **Evaluation of managers:** it provides the data needed to evaluate the performance of managers (e.g. heads of organizational units or cost centers). It makes it possible to define cost objectives and to link incentive systems (rewards and penalties) to their achievement, monitoring the performance trend of the individual units

## Practical Applications of Management Accounting in Managerial Decisions

The disaggregated cost information provided by management accounting is crucial for a wide range of strategic and operating decisions that management has to take.

**Example 1 (make or buy):**
A company must decide whether to keep producing a component internally or to entrust its production to an external supplier (outsourcing).

**Calculation Steps:**
- To make this decision, management must compare the internal production cost of the component with the purchase price from the external supplier
- Management accounting is indispensable because it provides a detailed analysis of all the costs incurred internally for the production of that good, allowing an accurate comparison and an informed choice

**Example 2 (pricing):**
Management must set the selling price of a new product.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Although the price is influenced by market demand, the company must know the production cost of the individual good or service
- Management accounting makes it possible to estimate this cost precisely, thus defining a minimum price below which the sale would generate a loss

**Example 3 (product profitability):**
A company produces three product lines (A, B, C) and wants to understand which one contributes most to the profit for the year.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Without disaggregated data, management only knows the overall profit. Management accounting makes it possible to allocate costs and revenues to each product line
- In this way, it is possible to calculate the profitability of each product and decide, for example, whether to eliminate a loss-making line or to push the production of the most profitable one

**Example 4 (production mix):**
Given a limited production capacity, the company must decide the quantities to produce for each good (A, B, C) in a given period.

**Calculation Steps:**
- This choice, called the "production mix", requires analysis of the specific costs tied to the production of A, B and C
- Knowing the costs and profitability of each product (thanks to management accounting), management can determine the mix that maximizes the overall economic result

## The Definition of Cost in Management Accounting

Cost is defined as the economic value that measures the consumption of the resources used for the specific activities aimed at achieving a predetermined objective.

**Explanation:**
This general definition allows great flexibility. The "predetermined objective" can be a product, a service, a process or an organizational unit (cost center). The management accounting system must measure the consumption of resources at the most elementary level (the single activity) and then aggregate these elementary costs. For example, to calculate the cost of "product A", the values of all the resources consumed in the different activities needed to make it are added up. If the object of analysis is "production center X", the costs of the resources used for the activities carried out inside that center are aggregated. In essence, while the income statement adds up all the resources consumed by the company, management accounting disaggregates and re-aggregates them according to the object of managerial analysis.
