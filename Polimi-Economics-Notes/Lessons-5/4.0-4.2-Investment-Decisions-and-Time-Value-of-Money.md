# Lessons 4.0-4.2: Investment Decisions and the Time Value of Money

## Topic Overview

This lesson introduces the fundamental concepts for assessing the economic convenience of company investments. It starts from the distinction between long-term strategic decisions and short-term tactical decisions, focusing on the former. An investment is defined as a project that, in exchange for an initial outlay, generates uncertain future cash flows. A model of how the company works is presented, first simplified (equity only) and then more realistic (with the addition of debt capital), to illustrate how investments generate financial flows. The lesson outlines the phases of management's decision-making process and the calculation methodologies (DCF and non-DCF), and stresses the importance of the time value of money, explaining how to discount future cash flows to make them comparable with today's outlays and analyzing the factors that influence the discount rate.

---

## 1. Types of Business Decisions

### Distinction between strategic and tactical decisions

Business decisions are divided into two main categories according to the time horizon of their effects.

- **Strategic or investment decisions:** generate effects in the long term, changing the company's overall setup and fundamental choices
- **Tactical decisions:** produce effects observable in the short term and do not change the company's competitive setup

### Short-term decisions (tactical)

- They do not change the total of the resources invested or the medium-to-long-term strategies
- Examples: determination of the break-even point, definition of the optimal production mix, "Make or Buy" choices

### Long-term decisions (strategic and investment)

- They have the effect of changing the company's overall setup
- They generate effects over a multi-year time horizon
- In the course, the terms "investment decisions" and "strategic decisions" are used as synonyms

## 2. The Concept of Investment

### Definition of investment

**A project that, in exchange for an initial tying-up of resources, generates income (cash flows) in the future.**

**Such income must be sufficient to remunerate the resources invested and to justify the risk.**

### Essential components of an investment

- **Initial outlay of resources:** tying up a significant amount of resources at the start
- **Future returns:** ability to generate economic results over a medium-to-long-term horizon
- **Uncertainty and risk:** future results are uncertain, making the investment inherently risky
- **Irreversibility:** the investment cannot be undone immediately without economic consequences

### Examples of company investments

Purchase or replacement of plants, automation projects, technological upgrading, expansion of the offering with new products.

---

## 3. Model of How the Company Works and Financial Flows

### Simplified scheme (equity only)

Shareholders contribute capital, the company uses it for investments (e.g. machinery), which generate cash flows, which are used to remunerate the shareholders (dividends).

### Complex model (with debt capital)

The company also resorts to financing from third parties (e.g. banks) to finance the investments.

Part of the cash flow generated must be used to remunerate the lenders (interest) and to repay the borrowed capital.

## 4. Assessing the Economic Convenience of an Investment

### Management's decision-making process

1. Identification of the decision alternatives
2. Verification of the consistency of the alternatives
3. Identification of the boundaries of the project analysis
4. Analysis of the competitive impacts
5. Economic evaluation through the calculation of the financial flows

### Financial perspective and calculation of cash flows

The evaluation focuses on monetary manifestations (cash inflows and outflows), excluding non-monetary costs such as depreciation.

- **Operating cash flow (CF):** difference between revenues and monetary operating costs
- **Investment (I):** cash flow tied to the acquisition/sale of assets (property, plants, patents, changes in working capital)
- **Net cash flow (NCF):** the difference between the operating cash flow and the investment in a given period (NCF = CF − I)

### Calculation methodologies

- **DCF (Discounted Cash Flow) methods:** consider the time value of money, discounting future cash flows. Examples: Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR)
- **Non-DCF methods:** do not take into account the time value of money

---

## 5. The Time Value of Money and Discounting

### Fundamental principle

Money changes value over time. It is not possible to compare cash flows of different periods with a simple algebraic sum.

### Compounding and discounting

- **Compounding:** calculates the future value of a sum today. Formula: **M = S × (1 + i)^n**
- **Discounting:** calculates the present value of a future sum. It is the inverse process, essential for comparing future flows with today's outlay. Formula: **S = M / (1 + i)^n**

### Factors influencing the discount rate (i or k)

- **Inflation:** reduction of the purchasing power of money over time
- **Risk:** the need to compensate for the uncertainty of future returns
- **Cost of debt capital:** cost of obtaining borrowed money from third parties
- **Opportunity cost:** the return given up by choosing a project instead of an alternative (e.g. government bonds)
