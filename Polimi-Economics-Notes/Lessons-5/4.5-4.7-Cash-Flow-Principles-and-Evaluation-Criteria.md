# Lessons 4.5-4.7: Cash Flow Principles and Evaluation Criteria

## Topic Overview

These lessons illustrate the principles and methods for calculating the economic convenience of an investment, with a particular focus on the Net Present Value (NPV) criterion and other methods based on discounted cash flows (DCF). First, two key operating principles for determining cash flows are discussed: the **financial logic**, which requires considering only actual monetary flows and excluding non-monetary costs (e.g. depreciation), and the **incremental logic**, which requires evaluating only the differential flows generated by the investment compared with a base case, excluding sunk costs. The lessons then explore the four ways of calculating the NPV, which derive from the combination of two choices: whether or not to consider the financing policy (shareholder logic vs. invested capital logic) and whether or not to include the tax impact (calculation before vs. after taxes). Finally, other evaluation criteria are presented, such as the Profitability Index (PI), the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) and the Discounted Payback Time, explaining their calculation and decision criteria.

## 1. Fundamental Principles for Calculating Cash Flows

### Reference principles for the calculation

- The lessons introduce the operating principles for calculating the economic convenience of an investment, fundamental for applying the Net Present Value
- The two cornerstone principles are the financial logic and the incremental logic

### Financial logic

- It establishes that only financial events (monetary flows) must be considered, not economic events
- Only revenues and costs with an associated actual cash flow are included in the calculation
- Non-monetary costs such as depreciation and provisions are excluded because they do not involve a cash outflow
- The cash flow is the difference between monetary revenues and monetary costs

### Incremental logic

- It requires considering only the differential financial flows generated by the investment compared with an alternative, defined as the "base case" (often, not investing)
- The relevant flow is the difference between the flow of the "investment" case and that of the "base case"

**Example:**
Replacing an old plant (variable cost €20) with a new one (variable cost €10), the incremental flow is the difference between the margins of the two scenarios.

### Importance of evaluating the base case

- A common mistake is to assume that, without investing, current performance remains constant. It is crucial to consider the possible future deterioration of performance in the base case

### Sunk costs and avoidable costs

- The incremental logic requires disregarding sunk costs, that is, costs already incurred and not recoverable (e.g. the cost of a feasibility study already paid)
- The evaluation must consider only the avoidable costs, that is, those actually affected by the investment decision. A labor saving is an avoidable cost only if it translates into a real cost reduction (e.g. layoffs, reduced overtime) or into an increase in productivity

## 2. Calculation Logics of the Net Present Value (NPV)

### Introduction to the NPV calculation logics

The NPV can be calculated in four ways, deriving from the combination of two choices:

1. **Financing policy:** equity (shareholder) logic vs. invested capital logic
2. **Tax impact:** calculation before taxes vs. calculation after taxes

### Tax impact (before vs. after taxes)

- **Calculation before taxes:** ignores the tax effect. Flow = Revenues − incremental monetary costs
- **Calculation after taxes:** the preferable approach, which considers the impact of taxes. Net flow = Cash flow − differential taxes. Taxes are calculated on the profit, which also takes into account non-monetary costs (depreciation)

### Calculation of the terminal value (V of T)

- **Before taxes:** coincides with the estimated market value of the asset at the end of the analysis period
- **After taxes:** taxation on the capital gain must be considered. Net terminal value = Market value − (Capital gain × Tax rate), where the capital gain is the difference between the market value and the book value

### Details on the financing logics

**Shareholder logic:**

1. Evaluates the project from the shareholders' point of view
2. The investment (I(t)) considers only the equity share
3. The discount rate is the cost of equity

**Invested capital logic:**

1. Evaluates the project from the point of view of the company as a whole
2. The investment (I(t)) includes both equity and third-party capital
3. The discount rate is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which considers the cost of equity and of debt. If calculated after taxes, the cost of debt must be corrected for the tax shield

## 3. Other Evaluation Criteria Based on Discounted Cash Flows (DCF)

### Profitability Index (PI)

- It is the ratio between the discounted cash flows and the discounted investments: PI = (NPV / Σ discounted investments) + 1
- **Decision criterion:** an investment is convenient if PI ≥ 1. It is an index of relative convenience (return per euro invested)

### Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

- It is the discount rate that makes the NPV of a project equal to zero
- **Decision criterion:** an investment is convenient if its IRR is higher than the cost of capital (k)

### Discounted Payback Time

- It represents the time needed for a project's cumulative NPV to become positive
- **Decision criterion:** an investment is acceptable if its time horizon is longer than the payback time; otherwise it does not recover the initial outlay while generating an adequate return
