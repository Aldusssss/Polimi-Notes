# Lessons 3.2-3.3: Cost Classification, Costing Systems and Cost Allocation

## Topic Overview

Full cost, allocation of indirect costs, controlling. The lessons introduce management accounting and the costing system used to determine the costs of activities, processes, products and organizational units. They explain the classifications (direct/indirect, product/period, fixed/variable), the cost configurations (prime cost, conversion cost, full industrial/company cost) and the income statement by nature/function. The allocation of indirect costs to full cost, with appropriate bases and drivers, is central. Direct vs. full costing and historical vs. standard costs are compared, illustrating rules, operating steps and managerial impacts.

## Definition of Cost and Levels of Analysis/Aggregation

Cost is the value or aggregate of values that measures the consumption of the resources used for activities aimed at achieving a predetermined objective (carrying out a process, good or service). It can be analyzed at the elementary level (single activity) and aggregated by process, product/service or organizational unit, depending on management's information needs.

**Key Points:**
- Cost as a measure of resource consumption for an objective (process/good/service)
- Analysis at the elementary level: single activity
- Aggregation by: process; product/service; organizational unit
- Purpose: provide targeted information to management for control and decisions

**Explanation:**
The lesson takes up the definition already given and links it to the figure shown (not included in the text) with two critical elements: granularity (elementary cost) and aggregation (cost object). The following technical analysis will deal with how to aggregate the elementary costs to the chosen cost object, but a classification of costs is presented first to guide this aggregation.

**Example:**
Management may request a cost analysis focused on the production of a specific product/service or on the set of costs of a given organizational unit. The choice affects which elementary costs are aggregated and how.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identification of the cost object (product/service or unit)
- Collection of the relevant elementary costs
- Attribution rules with classifications (direct/indirect; product/period)

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the cost object before aggregating
- Collect costs at the elementary level for analytical precision
- Choose the classification consistent with the managerial decisions required

## Classification: Direct Costs vs. Indirect Costs

A cost is direct if it can be attributed uniquely and unequivocally to a specific cost object (process, product, service, organizational unit, target). It is indirect if it cannot be attributed uniquely. The presence of indirect costs introduces the technical problem of allocation in order to determine the full cost.

**Key Points:**
- **Direct cost:** unique and unequivocal attribution to the cost object
- **Indirect cost:** no unique attribution; requires allocation
- Possible cost objects: process, product, service, organizational unit, target
- Impact on the methods for calculating full cost

**Explanation:**
The lesson insists on the rigorous definition of "unique and unequivocal". Direct costs are associated without ambiguity to the cost object; indirect ones require apportionment criteria. This is the "main technical theme" of the module: establishing allocation bases and drivers that allow shares of the indirect costs to be transferred to the cost objects.

**Example:**
A production process uses one machine to produce product codes A and B. The raw material used for A and for B is a direct cost because it is uniquely traceable to each product code. The cost of the machine (accounting depreciation) is an indirect cost because the machine is used for both A and B and is not uniquely attributable.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the direct components (raw materials specific to A and B)
- Identify the indirect components (depreciation of the shared machine)
- Establish the need to apportion the indirect cost to calculate full cost

**Considerations:**
- Apply rigorous attribution criteria to avoid distortions
- Accurately separate direct from indirect costs in data collection
- Document the cost object and the traceability of the direct costs

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a cost is shared among several objects but can be monitored through operating measures (e.g. machine hours), how should it be attributed? Use operating drivers for apportionment, as covered in later lessons

## Full Cost and Allocation of Indirect Costs

To determine the full cost of a product/service/cost object, a share of the indirect costs must be added to the direct costs. The share and the method of determining it are matters to be addressed with allocation techniques (deferred to later lessons).

**Key Points:**
- **Full cost** = direct costs + share of indirect costs
- The definition of the "share" is crucial and non-trivial
- Allocation enables informed decisions on price, margin, efficiency

**Explanation:**
In the example of products A and B, the full cost requires the attribution of part of the machine's depreciation. Without allocation, the full cost would be underestimated and managerial decisions (pricing, mix, investments) could be wrong.

**Example:**
The direct costs specific to A and B (raw materials, direct labor if any) are added up and a share of the machine's indirect cost is added. The choice of the share is left to technical criteria that will be illustrated in later lessons.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Collect the direct costs for A and B
- Identify the relevant indirect costs (depreciation of the shared machine)
- Apply an allocation criterion to attribute the share of depreciation

**Considerations:**
- Do not confuse general indirect costs with non-production period costs
- Verify the consistency of the apportionment criterion with the causal link of the costs

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the allocation drivers change over time, how should the update be handled? Redo the allocation with updated drivers to maintain accuracy

## Classification: Product Costs vs. Period Costs

Product costs are the resources used inside the production processes (direct labor, direct materials, production overhead). Period costs are the resources used in support activities not directly associable to the production process by a causal link (administration, legal, human resources, property management).

**Key Points:**
- **Product cost:** tied to the production processes; includes indirect production costs (overhead)
- **Period cost:** support activities not directly tied to production
- Example: depreciation of the machine for A and B is a product cost (even though it is indirect)
- The distinction affects managerial decisions and external accounting (financial statements)

**Explanation:**
Recalling the value chain, the primary processes generate product costs, while the support processes generate period costs. This classification guides cost containment choices and has effects on external accounting records.

**Example:**
Primary processes: activities directly tied to the production of goods/services. Support processes: administration, legal support, human resources management, property management. The costs of the support processes are classified as period costs.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map the business processes according to the value chain
- Assign the costs to the "product" or "period" categories based on the link with production
- Use this mapping to guide spending decisions and reporting

**Considerations:**
- Ensure a clear causal link to classify as a product cost
- Separate production overhead from administrative support costs
- Consider the effects on the financial statements in the classification

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a support activity has parts dedicated to the production process, how should it be classified? Split the production component as a product cost and the remainder as a period cost through time tracking or appropriate drivers

## Product Costs (Inventoriable Costs)

Product costs are those costs directly or indirectly tied to the production process and are inventoriable: they are absorbed into the valuation of inventories. They include direct materials (DM), direct labor (DL) and indirect production costs (production overhead, OVH). They can be associated step by step, activity by activity, with the good/service through management accounting, and when the good/service is not sold in the year, the related cost remains in inventories and does not affect the net operating margin of the year.

**Key Points:**
- They are inventoriable costs: they enter the value of closing inventories
- **Components:** direct materials (DM), direct labor (DL), indirect production costs (OVH)
- Allocable to the good/service through management accounting systems
- Impact on the income statement shifted to the period of sale through the cost of goods sold
- Basis for calculating prime cost, conversion costs, full industrial cost
- Examples of indirect production costs: production machinery, process energy, machine maintenance

**Explanation:**
Management accounting makes it possible to trace activities and phases, assigning DM, DL and OVH to the product. In the financial statements, these costs flow into inventories if the product is not sold. When it is sold, they add up in the cost of goods sold. This creates a bridge between the internal collection of data (by activity and product) and the external representation (income statement).

**Example:**
If the company bought raw materials and in year T uses 60 of them for goods/services produced in the year, 40 remain as inventories. These 40 are incorporated in the inventories and do not affect the net operating margin of year T. Only the share used in products sold enters the cost of goods sold.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the quantities of raw materials purchased in the year
- Determine the share actually used for goods sold in year T
- Assign the unsold share to closing inventories
- Reflect only the sold share in the cost of goods sold, leaving the net operating margin unchanged for the part in inventories

**Considerations:**
- Use management accounting systems to correctly assign DM, DL and OVH to products
- Ensure traceability of activities/phases for an accurate valuation of inventories
- Align internal assignment criteria with external financial statement criteria for consistency
- Monitor the distinction between product and period costs for a correct determination of the cost of goods sold

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a good/service remains unsold at the closing of year T, how is the cost handled? Incorporate it in closing inventories as an inventoriable cost and do not charge it to the net operating margin of year T

## Period Costs

Period costs are tied to the general and indirect functioning of the company (support activities, administration, sales, welfare). They cannot be uniquely assigned to the production process, are not inventoriable and are recorded directly in the income statement of the year in which they are incurred, affecting net operating margin and profit.

**Key Points:**
- Not directly assignable to individual products
- Not inventoriable: they go immediately into the income statement
- **Categories:** administration, offices, insurance on employees and non-industrial buildings, general expenses, sales, advertising, promotion, training, legal support, corporate welfare
- Difficult to allocate by product; for this reason decisions are often based on full industrial cost

**Explanation:**
The collection by nature (materials, personnel, services, depreciation) in the income statement includes the period costs directly in the year. The representation by function would isolate the cost of goods sold, but without internal accounting it cannot be determined. Management considers the coverage of the period costs with total revenues, while basing many analyses on the full industrial cost.

**Example:**
Salaries and travel expenses of internal sales agents, depreciation, insurance and operating expenses of the vehicles of salespeople/distributors. They do not take part in the production process but support the commercial activity.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the items related to the commercial function
- Classify them as period costs
- Record them by nature in the income statement of the year

**Considerations:**
- Clearly separate period costs from product costs to avoid wrong capitalizations
- Monitor discretionary expenses (advertising, promotion, training) that directly affect the profit for the year
- Assess prudent allocation policies so as not to artificially inflate inventories

**Special Circumstances:**
- If period costs have multi-year benefits, how are they handled? Even though they have future benefits, since they cannot be assigned to the production process, they are recorded in the year unless specific applicable capitalization rules exist

## Cost Configurations: Prime Cost, Conversion Costs, Full Industrial Cost, Full Company Cost

Different configurations respond to specific managerial needs. Prime cost = direct materials + direct labor. Conversion costs = direct labor + indirect production costs. Full industrial cost = DM + DL + OVH. Full company cost = full industrial cost + period costs.

**Key Points:**
- **Prime cost:** measures the part most directly associable to the good/service (DM, DL)
- **Conversion costs:** measure the transformation of DM into finished products (DL, OVH)
- **Full industrial cost:** sum of all product costs (DM, DL, OVH)
- **Full company cost:** also includes the period costs
- Predominant managerial use of full industrial cost for operating decisions

**Explanation:**
Management analyzes the prime cost vertically to understand the impact of materials and direct labor, and the conversion costs to assess transformation efficiency. For pricing and margins by product line the full industrial cost is used; the coverage of the period costs is planned at the overall company level.

**Example:**
To the question "what is the production cost of code A or of good B?", DM, DL and OVH are calculated for each. The sum gives the full industrial cost. Adding the period costs gives the full company cost.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Collect DM, DL and OVH for each code/good
- Add them up to obtain the full industrial cost
- Estimate the share of period costs to be covered by total revenues
- Assess decisions (pricing, mix) based on the full industrial cost

**Considerations:**
- Clearly define the allocation bases for OVH to minimize distortions
- Use prime cost and conversion costs for efficiency and make-or-buy analyses
- Avoid loading period costs onto products without robust criteria

**Special Circumstances:**
- If OVH is hard to allocate precisely, how should it proceed? Apply appropriate cost drivers and consider ABC systems to improve accuracy

## Income Statement: By Nature vs. By Function and Cost of Goods Sold

The income statement can be presented by nature (as provided for by the Civil Code) or by function (isolating the cost of goods sold). The cost of goods sold, in the analytical logic, is the sum of the product costs (consumption of raw materials, direct labor, indirect production costs). Without internal accounting, it is not possible to determine the cost of goods sold.

**Key Points:**
- **By nature:** aggregation by materials, personnel, services, depreciation, etc.
- **By function:** isolation of the cost of goods sold based on management accounting
- The cost of goods sold corresponds to the product costs related to the goods sold
- The distinction affects the preparation of the income statement and the valuation of inventories

**Explanation:**
The representation by function requires analytical traceability of the product costs assigned to the goods sold in the year. Costs not sold remain in inventories. Period costs enter directly among the costs of the year, reducing net operating margin and profit.

**Example:**
The consumption of raw materials, the direct labor and the indirect production costs related to the goods actually sold in year T are added up. Those related to goods not sold remain in inventories.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify the goods sold in year T
- Assign the related DM, DL, OVH
- Add them up for the cost of goods sold
- Exclude period costs from the cost of goods sold item

**Considerations:**
- Maintain consistency between internal accounting criteria and external reporting
- Update inventories accurately to avoid errors in the cost of goods sold

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the analytical data are incomplete, how should the income statement by function be presented? Avoid the representation by function and stick to the one by nature until the accuracy of the cost of goods sold is ensured

## Classification of Costs: Variable, Fixed and Semi-Variable/Semi-Fixed

A cost is variable if it varies as a function of the production volume (not necessarily proportionally); it is fixed if it does not vary as the production volume varies within a certain horizon and operating level. There are semi-variable/semi-fixed costs that increase in discrete steps when volume thresholds are exceeded (e.g. purchase of a second machine). Determination requires defining the time horizon and the operating volume.

**Key Points:**
- **Variable:** depends on production volume (e.g. raw materials, process energy)
- **Fixed:** does not depend on volume within a range (e.g. depreciation of the machine)
- **Semi-variable/semi-fixed:** varies in steps with capacity thresholds
- Importance of defining the time horizon (month, 2 months, 3 months, year) and the operating volume
- Graphical representation: total costs on the vertical axis, quantities produced on the horizontal axis

**Explanation:**
The behavior of costs is plotted on a quantity vs. costs graph to identify linearity or steps. For depreciation, fixity holds within the capacity of one machine; beyond that, a new investment is needed and total depreciation changes. The variable cost grows with quantity; the fixed cost stays flat; the semi-variable shows steps.

**Example:**
Variable product costs: direct materials, direct labor, energy. Variable period costs: sales commissions, shipping expenses. Fixed product costs: depreciation of machinery, rent of production warehouses, indirect labor with a fixed salary, maintenance of production machinery. Fixed period costs: administrative expenses, R&D expenses, advertising and promotion not tied to individual goods/services.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Distinguish the link with the production process vs. support/sales functions
- Classify the behavior with respect to volume
- Verify the time horizon and the capacity to confirm the nature of the cost

**Considerations:**
- Define the capacity range and the horizon to avoid errors in classification
- Update the classification when technology or volumes change
- Separate product costs from period costs also in the variable/fixed classification

**Special Circumstances:**
- If production exceeds existing capacity, how does the nature of the cost change? Total depreciation may increase due to the purchase of new machinery, turning the curve from fixed to semi-fixed with a step

## Costing System: Definition and Purpose

The costing system is the set of techniques that allow a company's management to determine analytically the costs of activities, processes, products, services and organizational units. It is part of management accounting and makes it possible to obtain the cost of producing specific goods and services (final cost objects).

**Key Points:**
- Enables the analytical determination of the costs of activities, processes, products, services, organizational units
- It is instrumental to management accounting for defining costs by function
- Supports managerial decisions on pricing, efficiency, management control
- Requires careful design of the collection and processing of economic-financial data

**Explanation:**
In the context of the lesson, the costing system is introduced as the information architecture that makes it possible to measure costs analytically. Its primary function is to provide management with reliable information on the costs of goods and services, considering the underlying activities and processes and the organizational units that consume resources. The system works by distinguishing the classification by nature (chart of accounts) from the placement by function (cost centers and cost objects).

**Example:**
It starts from a chart of accounts with items such as "raw material X", "raw material Y", "salaries for production bonuses", "salaries for office staff". Step 1: assign each item to the responsible cost center (e.g. department 1 for raw materials, finance office for office staff salaries). Step 2: allocate the costs of the centers to the final products/services. Example: the cost of the raw materials used in department 1 is attributed directly to product/service code 111. Indirect and period costs require specific allocation rules.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identification of the cost items in the chart of accounts
- Mapping to the cost centers (productive and non-productive)
- Allocation to the products/services with methods appropriate for direct and indirect costs

**Considerations:**
- The precise definition of the elementary cost items is essential to avoid dispersion of information
- The chart of accounts must be disaggregated to the minimum level of activity to improve precision
- The mere existence of the chart of accounts is not enough: cost centers and cost objects are needed
- The attribution and allocation rules must be formalized for direct, indirect and period costs
- Correctly distinguish between productive and non-productive centers
- Correctly associate the set of resources to the product and/or organizational unit
- The graphical representation of the flow (items → centers → products) helps the design
- Information on the costs of organizational units is useful even before the costs of products/services

**Special Circumstances:**
- If costs that are not directly attributable to a product (indirect costs) are encountered, how should they be handled? Define appropriate allocation drivers based on the activities of the cost centers
- If a cost item belongs to several cost centers, how should it be handled? Establish objective apportionment criteria (e.g. working hours, square meters, resource consumption)
- If the chart of accounts is not sufficiently disaggregated, how should it be handled? Refine the list down to the minimum level of activity needed for the desired precision

## Architecture of the Costing System: Three Pillars

The design of a costing system rests on three fundamental elements: 1) chart of accounts (elementary cost items), 2) plan of cost centers (organizational units responsible for the use of resources), 3) products and services (final cost objects).

**Key Points:**
- **Chart of accounts:** list of cost items by nature, disaggregated to the minimum level of activity
- **Plan of cost centers:** definition of the organizational units responsible for the use of resources and the generation of costs
- **Products and services:** final cost objects towards which costs are directed and aggregated

**Explanation:**
The lesson emphasizes that without a chart of accounts the information on costs would be dispersed; however, the chart of accounts alone is not sufficient, because it only provides the classification by nature. Cost centers (by function) must be defined, distinguishing between productive centers (production of final goods/services) and non-productive ones (support: sales, purchasing, finance, administration and control, personnel management). Finally, the final cost objects, that is, products and services, are the ultimate aim of the analysis. The integration of these three pillars makes it possible to collect and process the economic-financial data correctly.

**Example:**
Items: "raw material X", "raw material Y", "salaries for production bonuses", "salaries for office staff". Centers: productive (e.g. department 1), non-productive (sales office, purchasing office, finance office, administration and control, personnel management). Final objects: product/service with code 111 and others.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Creation of the list of items by nature
- Identification of the responsible centers
- Linking of the final cost objects for the allocation phase

**Considerations:**
- Consistency between the chart of accounts and the plan of cost centers
- Adequate granularity of the items to improve traceability
- Clarity in the perimeter of the centers (productive vs. non-productive)
- Explicit definition of the final cost objects
- Documentation of the relationships items-centers-products

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a center carries out both productive and support activities, how should it be handled? Separate sub-centers or apply apportionment criteria based on the prevailing activities

## Process of Attribution and Allocation of Costs

The determination of the cost of products and services follows two steps: Step 1 – assign the cost items to the cost centers; Step 2 – attribute/allocate the costs of the cost centers to the final products and services, according to the activities carried out for their production.

**Key Points:**
- **Step 1:** each item of the chart of accounts must be referred to a specific cost center
- **Step 2:** the costs of the cost centers are allocated to the final products/services
- **Simple cases:** direct costs (raw materials) attributable directly to the product/service code (e.g. 111)
- **Complex cases:** indirect production costs and period costs of the non-productive centers require allocation rules

**Explanation:**
The lesson explains that for some costs (e.g. raw materials of department 1) the attribution to product/service 111 is direct. For indirect and period costs, allocation is harder since they are not uniquely referable. It is therefore necessary to define rules of recording, attribution and allocation to transfer the costs from the centers to the products consistently with the activities carried out.

**Example:**
Direct: "raw material X" consumed in department 1 attributed to product 111. Indirect: "salaries for office staff" of the administration and control office to be distributed over all products with established criteria (e.g. support hours, transaction volumes).

**Calculation Steps:**
- Identify direct and indirect costs
- Apply allocation rules for indirect and period costs
- Document the allocation drivers based on the activities of the centers

**Considerations:**
- Verify the traceability of the items to the centers
- Choose allocation drivers consistent with the activities
- Manage period costs separately from production costs
- Validate the accuracy of the allocations with sensitivity analysis

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the allocation drivers change over time, how should it be handled? Periodically update the rules and recalculate the allocations to maintain accuracy

## Rules of Recording, Attribution and Allocation

In addition to the data map (chart of accounts, cost centers, cost objects), rules must be defined on which resources to associate with products and/or organizational units and how to allocate indirect and period costs.

**Key Points:**
- First rule stated: define the set of resources to associate with the product and/or the organizational unit
- Need for rules for the allocation of indirect production costs and period costs
- The costing system must also allow the determination of the costs of organizational units

**Explanation:**
The lesson indicates as indispensable the definition of rules that determine which items (resources) are aggregated on specific products or organizational units and with which criteria they are moved from the centers to the products. This includes the choice of allocation drivers based on the activities of the cost centers and the distinction between direct, indirect and period costs.

**Example:**
For the product with code 111, raw materials X and Y and the costs of department 1 are associated directly; shares of the costs of the sales office and the finance office are associated indirectly according to pre-established rules.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Map the resources that are direct to the product
- Define drivers for the indirect costs (e.g. labor hours, orders, square meters)
- Apply the rules consistently and with adequate documentation

**Considerations:**
- Clarity in defining the perimeter of the resources to associate
- Alignment of the rules with the operating reality of the activities and processes
- Periodic checks on the correctness of the rules and of the results
- Integration with general accounting for data consistency

**Special Circumstances:**
- If new products/services emerge, how should it be handled? Update the set of resources and the allocation rules, adding new codes and centers if necessary

## Direct Costing vs. Full Costing

Two approaches to the determination of costs. Direct costing attributes to products or organizational units only the directly referable costs. Full costing passes on to final products and services all the costs of the company, including those not directly referable to the final good/service. The configurations are not alternatives: it is possible to determine both the full cost and the direct cost. Direct costing neglects indirect costs; full costing requires allocation rules to distribute the indirect costs.

**Key Points:**
- Direct costing considers only direct costs
- Full costing also includes indirect costs and period costs
- The two configurations coexist and serve different managerial purposes
- In full costing, allocation rules for indirect costs must be established
- In direct costing, indirect costs are not allocated

**Explanation:**
In the costing system, the choice between direct and full costing depends on the managerial objective. For short-term margin analyses direct costing is preferred, while for assessments of overall cost, pricing and profitability by product full costing is used. The two configurations can be calculated in parallel to provide complementary views. The determination of full cost implies the inclusion of a share of the indirect costs, whose allocation must be defined with explicit criteria (causal or proportional).

**Considerations:**
- Clarify the objective of the analysis before choosing the approach (direct vs. full)
- Make the attribution/allocation rules explicit
- Avoid mixing criteria without transparency
- Communicate the limitations of the causal criterion on indirect costs
- Document the allocation bases chosen

**Special Circumstances:**
- If indirect costs cannot be attributed causally, how should it be handled? Use a proportional criterion tied to the consumption of a relevant resource (machine time, volume, etc.)

## Valuation of Costs: Historical Costs vs. Standard Costs

The valuation of resources can be done using historical costs or standard costs. Historical costs are those actually incurred and recorded by management accounting. Standard costs are estimates of the optimal cost under conditions of efficiency of the production system for specific cost items. Companies frequently estimate standard costs for different natures, activities, processes, products and services, and constantly monitor the variance between historical and standard costs.

**Key Points:**
- Historical costs = costs actually incurred
- Standard costs = estimate of the optimal cost under conditions of efficiency
- Continuous comparison between historical and standard for management control
- Need for precise management accounting for systematic measurement
- Variance analysis as a typical function of management control

**Explanation:**
To use standard costs, efficiency benchmarks are defined for materials, labor, machinery and processes. Management accounting records historical costs by activity, process, product, organizational unit. Management control compares historical and standard, analyzes variances in volume, price and efficiency, and identifies corrective actions. This process supports budgeting, pricing and continuous improvement.

**Considerations:**
- Define the standards for each cost item in a realistic and up-to-date way
- Collect historical data with sufficient granularity (activities, processes, products)
- Implement constant monitoring of the variances
- Interpret the variances by distinguishing between price, volume and efficiency
- Align the standard costs with the efficiency objectives of the production system

**Special Circumstances:**
- If reliable historical data are missing, how should it be handled? Resort to standard estimates based on engineering analyses and benchmarks, and strengthen data collection to improve precision over time

## Attribution vs. Allocation of Costs

In the language of the course, "attribution" is used when a cost can be traced directly to the cost object. "Allocation" is used when the cost is indirect and must be apportioned among cost objects with non-direct criteria. The allocation of indirect costs requires explicit criteria and a suitable allocation base.

**Key Points:**
- **Attribution:** cost directly associable to the cost object
- **Allocation:** indirect cost apportioned through a criterion
- Choice between causal and proportional criteria
- **Allocation base** = amount of the resource used to distribute the cost
- Step-by-step process for proportional allocation

**Explanation:**
The cost objects are defined, the indirect cost items to be allocated are identified, an allocation base is chosen for each item (e.g. machine hours, square meters, number of orders), an allocation coefficient is calculated by dividing the total cost by the total base, and the coefficient is applied to the consumption of the individual products to obtain the allocated shares.

**Example:**
The production department of company X uses the same machine for A, B and C. Monthly depreciation (indirect fixed cost) = €5,000. Machine hours: A = 15 hours, B = 10 hours, C = 25 hours. Allocation base: total machine hours = 50 hours. Allocation coefficient = €5,000 / 50 hours = €100 per machine hour. Allocated shares: A = €100/hour × 15 hours = €1,500, B = €100/hour × 10 hours = €1,000, C = €100/hour × 25 hours = €2,500. The apportionment is a function of the allocation base (machine time) and is not uniform among A, B and C.

**Calculation Steps:**
- Determine the cost object: products A, B, C
- Identify the indirect cost to be allocated: monthly depreciation of the machine = €5,000
- Choose the allocation base: machine hours
- Calculate the total base: 15 + 10 + 25 = 50 hours
- Calculate the allocation coefficient: €5,000 / 50 hours = €100/hour
- Allocate to each product: A = €1,500, B = €1,000, C = €2,500
- Verify that the sum of the allocated shares = €5,000

**Considerations:**
- Make explicit and document the allocation base chosen for each indirect cost
- Verify the consistency between the allocation base and the driver of consumption of the resource
- Avoid the use of unrepresentative bases that distort product costs
- Periodically recalibrate the coefficients if volumes or the production mix change

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the consumption driver changes (e.g. new technology reduces machine hours), how should it be handled? Update the allocation base and recalculate the coefficients to maintain the accuracy of the allocation

## Criteria for Allocating Indirect Costs: Causal vs. Proportional

Two macro-criteria: causal (attributing costs to the resources specifically consumed by each product) and proportional (distributing costs in proportion to the consumption of a chosen resource). The causal criterion is often hard to apply to costs that are indirect by nature; proportional criteria are therefore used, with bases such as machine time, production volumes, etc.

**Key Points:**
- **Causal criterion:** theoretically more rigorous but often impracticable for indirect costs
- **Proportional criterion:** pragmatic, based on a measurable resource
- The choice of allocation base strongly influences the cost shares
- The base must be measured and aggregated correctly (sum S)

**Explanation:**
S is defined as the sum of the consumption of the allocation base for all products. Q is defined as the total value of the indirect cost to be allocated. The allocation coefficient = Q / S is calculated. For each product i, the share Q_i = (Q / S) × S_i, where S_i is the consumption of the base by product i. This operating rule allows a transparent proportional apportionment.

**Considerations:**
- Select bases that causally reflect consumption, even if a proportional criterion is used
- Test the sensitivity of the results to different allocation bases
- Communicate the impact of the production mix on the shares of indirect costs

**Special Circumstances:**
- If several bases are relevant for the same indirect cost, how should it be handled? Split the cost into components and allocate each component with the most appropriate base

## Proportional Allocation Process in Three Phases

The process of proportional allocation of indirect costs is divided into three phases: (1) Determination of the cost object and of the indirect cost items to be allocated; (2) Choice of the allocation base for each indirect cost item; (3) Allocation of the costs to the different products through the allocation coefficient.

**Key Points:**
- **Phase 1:** definition of cost objects and indirect cost items
- **Phase 2:** choice of the allocation base for each item
- **Phase 3:** calculation of the coefficient and allocation of the shares to the products

**Explanation:**
Operationally: collect the consumption of the base for each product; sum to obtain S; record Q for the cost item; calculate the coefficient = Q/S; multiply the coefficient by S_i to obtain Q_i; verify that the sum of the Q_i equals Q.

**Considerations:**
- Verify the quality of the base consumption data
- Ensure traceability between production data and management accounting
- Carry out balancing checks on the allocated sums

**Special Circumstances:**
- If the allocation base data are incomplete, how should it be handled? Supplement with reasonable documented estimates and flag the areas of uncertainty for future review

## Cost of Organizational Units

The cost of an organizational unit, in the framework of management accounting, can be defined in a Direct version (resources directly used) or in a broader version that also includes a share of the period costs of the support units that allow it to function. To calculate the overall cost of operation, logics similar to the allocation of indirect costs seen for products are applied.

**Key Points:**
- **Direct cost of the unit:** resources directly used
- **Full cost of the unit:** includes shares of period costs of the support units
- Application of the allocation criteria to the support costs
- Analogous decisions: which costs to include, how to value them (historical vs. standard), how to allocate them

**Explanation:**
Management can allocate period costs (overhead of the support functions) to the production units using bases such as labor hours, machine hours, square meters, number of orders. The purpose is clearly defined (budget, internal pricing, benchmarking), the appropriate base is chosen, the coefficient is calculated and the shares are apportioned. The process repeats the decisions seen for products and services.

**Considerations:**
- Clarify for which decisions the unit's cost is calculated (budget, performance, internal pricing)
- Select bases aligned with the support activities
- Periodically update the allocation based on organizational changes

**Special Circumstances:**
- If a unit receives shared services that are not tracked, how should it be handled? Apply documented proxy bases and plan the introduction of more granular recording systems
